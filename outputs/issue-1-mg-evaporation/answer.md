Question: Quantitative magnesium evaporation loss during laser powder bed fusion (LPBF / SLM) of AlSi10Mg.

Context: Mg has a boiling point (1091 C) below the melting point of Si (1414 C) and far below LPBF melt-pool peak temperatures, so selective Mg vaporization is expected. Our feedstock powder contains 0.05-0.07 wt% Mg (below the nominal 0.20-0.45 wt% AlSi10Mg specification), and we want to predict the Mg content in the as-built part.

Questions to answer with quantitative literature data:
1. What fractional/relative Mg loss (percent of initial Mg content, or absolute wt% drop) has been measured between AlSi10Mg powder and as-built LPBF parts? Cite specific studies with measured powder vs. as-built Mg wt% values and the measurement technique (ICP-OES, EDS, XRF, etc.).
2. How does Mg loss depend on process parameters (laser power, scan speed, volumetric energy density, hatch spacing) and machine atmosphere (Ar flow, chamber pressure)? Is there a model (e.g., Langmuir evaporation-based) that predicts element loss in LPBF, and what does it predict for Mg in Al alloys?
3. Does fractional Mg loss depend on the initial Mg concentration (i.e., is relative loss roughly constant so results from 0.3-0.4 wt% powders can be scaled to a 0.05-0.07 wt% powder)?
4. Based on the literature, what Mg wt% range should be expected in as-built parts if the powder contains 0.05-0.07 wt% Mg under typical AlSi10Mg LPBF parameters (roughly 200-400 W, 1000-1600 mm/s, Ar atmosphere)?

Provide numeric estimates with uncertainty and cite the primary sources.

# Quantitative Magnesium Evaporation Loss During LPBF of AlSi10Mg

## 1. Measured Fractional/Relative Mg Loss: Powder vs. As-Built Composition

Direct quantitative measurements of Mg loss between powder feedstock and as-built LPBF parts are surprisingly scarce for AlSi10Mg specifically, though several closely related studies provide strong constraints.

**AlSi10Mg and AlSi7Mg0.6 (Low-Mg Al-Si alloys):** Multiple studies of AlSi10Mg powder recycling have found that the main alloying element composition—including Mg—does not change significantly during LPBF. Weiss et al. (2022) performed ICP-AES analysis of AlSi10Mg powder over 10 recycling cycles on an SLM280HL (up to 400 W, 60 µm layer thickness, Ar atmosphere) and reported "no major change" in the main constituents of the powder, including Mg (weiss2022ontheinfluence pages 4-5). Maamoun et al. (2018) measured AlSi10Mg powder composition by EDS and found fresh powder contained 0.37 wt% Mg while recycled powder contained 0.34 wt% Mg—a difference of only ~0.03 wt%, within EDS uncertainty (maamoun2018thermalpostprocessingof pages 3-5). Smolina et al. (2022) performed XRF analysis on both AlSi7Mg0.6 powder and LPBF-fabricated bulk specimens across five reuse cycles. The powder Mg content ranged from 0.61–0.72 wt%, while the as-built LPBF specimens measured 0.715–0.735 wt% Mg. The authors concluded that "the magnesium content in the alloy did not change significantly in the case of both the powder and solid samples" (smolina2022influenceofthe pages 8-11). These results suggest that for Al-Si alloys with ≤0.7 wt% Mg processed under typical LPBF conditions, the **net measurable Mg loss is negligible to very small (<5%)** within the resolution of EDS/XRF techniques.

**Al-Zn-Mg alloys (Higher-Mg systems):** The most quantitative direct powder-to-part comparison comes from Babu et al. (2020), who measured chemical composition by ICP-AES for three custom Al-Zn-Mg alloys processed by LPBF at 95 W and 175 mm/s. The Mg content decreased from 2.3 → 2.1 wt% (8.7% relative loss) for Al-10Zn-2Mg, from 3.34 → 2.79 wt% (16.5% relative loss) for Al-14Zn-3Mg, and from 4.40 → 3.28 wt% (25.5% relative loss) for Al-18Zn-4Mg (babu2020laserpowderbed media 7a4d691d). The latter alloy also exhibited cracking attributed to the appreciable evaporative loss of both Zn and Mg (babu2020laserpowderbed pages 4-7).

**Al-Mg-Sc (Scalmalloy®):** Electromagnetic levitation (EML) experiments by Bärtl et al. (2022) represent an upper-bound reference case. When Scalmalloy® (~4.9 wt% Mg) was superheated to T_L + 540 K for approximately 45 seconds in EML processing, the Langmuir-based prediction and experimental measurement showed **100% Mg loss**—all 9.056 × 10⁻³ g of Mg evaporated completely (bartl2022influenceofsurface media 4e1d16d5, bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 10-10). The total alloy mass loss was 3.75% actual (3.01% predicted), dominated by Mg evaporation. While this is not a direct LPBF experiment, it confirms that Mg has the highest evaporation propensity among alloying elements in Al-Mg alloys.

## 2. Process Parameter and Atmosphere Dependence

**Energy density:** Vaporization of alloying elements intensifies with increasing volumetric energy density (VED). Shi et al. (2024), in a comprehensive review, noted that higher energy density inputs more heat to the melt pool, leading to higher peak temperatures and more intensified vaporization, with scanning speed having a greater impact on metal vaporization than laser power alone (shi2024areviewof pages 17-21). This is consistent with the Langmuir model, in which evaporation flux increases exponentially with temperature through the vapor pressure term.

**Langmuir evaporation model:** The theoretical framework for predicting element loss in LPBF is well-established. Mukherjee and DebRoy (2016) showed that the vaporization flux J_i of alloying element i can be estimated from the Langmuir equation: J_i = λP_i / √(2πM_iT), where P_i is the vapor pressure, M_i is the molecular weight, T is the melt pool temperature, and λ is a condensation factor (mukherjee2016printabilityofalloys pages 4-6). Bärtl et al. (2022) extended this with an activity-based formulation: (1/M_i)(dm_i/dt) = a_i·P_{v,i}·A_s / √(2πM_iR_GT), where a_i = γ_i·c_i is the activity (activity coefficient × concentration) (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 8-10). Critically, a shielding factor α = 1/(1 + K·P_G^n) accounts for the suppression of evaporation by the inert gas atmosphere: with K = 0.012 and n = 1.0 for metallic elements in Ar, at typical LPBF chamber pressure (~750 mbar), α ≈ 1.11 × 10⁻³, meaning the Ar atmosphere reduces evaporation by approximately three orders of magnitude compared to vacuum (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 8-10). The vapor pressure P_{v,i} follows Alcock's four-term equation and increases strongly with temperature. For Mg, the boiling point of 1091°C is far below typical melt pool peak temperatures (>2000°C near the laser spot), making it the most volatile species in Al-Si-Mg alloys (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 5-7, bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 10-11).

**Atmosphere effects:** The Ar shielding gas plays a dual role: (i) providing an inert environment and (ii) dramatically suppressing evaporation kinetics. Helium provides weaker shielding, with evaporation rates approximately 2× faster than in Ar for elements with molar mass >20 g/mol (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 8-10). Higher gas flow rates can remove evaporation fume more efficiently from the melt zone (qin2021influenceoflaser pages 7-10), but the primary suppression comes from gas pressure.

## 3. Concentration Dependence of Fractional Mg Loss

The Langmuir model predicts that the evaporation mass flux of element i is proportional to its activity a_i = γ_i·x_i, where x_i is the mole fraction in the melt and γ_i is the activity coefficient (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 8-10, mukherjee2016printabilityofalloys pages 4-6). For an ideal dilute solution (γ_i ≈ constant), the absolute mass evaporation rate scales linearly with concentration, which implies that the **fractional (relative) loss should be approximately independent of initial concentration** when the melt pool thermal history is held constant.

However, the experimental data from Babu et al. (2020) show that fractional Mg loss increases with initial Mg content: 8.7% at 2.3 wt%, 16.5% at 3.34 wt%, and 25.5% at 4.40 wt% Mg (babu2020laserpowderbed media 7a4d691d). This apparent increase likely reflects that the higher-Mg alloys also had substantially higher Zn content (10.8–19.2 wt%), and the combined evaporative cooling effects, different melt pool dynamics, and non-ideal thermodynamic behavior all contribute. Additionally, in the Bärtl et al. (2022) EML experiments, the very high Mg content (~4.9 wt%) in Scalmalloy enabled complete Mg depletion, illustrating that at high initial concentrations, the Mg activity is sufficient to drive very rapid evaporation (bartl2022influenceofsurface media 4e1d16d5).

For AlSi10Mg with only 0.20–0.45 wt% Mg (specification range), or even lower at 0.05–0.07 wt% Mg, the Mg mole fraction is very small (~0.05–0.15 at.%), and the absolute evaporation driving force is correspondingly weak. The Langmuir framework suggests that if the relative loss at 0.3–0.4 wt% Mg is already below the detection limits of EDS/XRF (i.e., ≲5%), then at 0.05–0.07 wt% the relative loss should be comparable or less. The key insight is that the thermal history of the melt pool is primarily dictated by the Al-Si matrix (not the trace Mg), so reducing Mg content does not significantly change the evaporative conditions, but it does reduce the total mass of Mg available per melt pool, making the absolute loss smaller.

**Best estimate:** Under typical AlSi10Mg LPBF parameters (200–400 W, 1000–1600 mm/s, Ar atmosphere, ~30–60 µm layers), the expected Mg content in as-built parts from a 0.05–0.07 wt% Mg powder is approximately **0.045–0.070 wt% Mg**, corresponding to 0–10% relative loss. The central expectation is near-complete Mg retention, based on the following reasoning:

1. Studies on AlSi10Mg recycling (Weiss et al. 2022; Maamoun et al. 2018) and AlSi7Mg0.6 bulk/powder comparisons (Smolina et al. 2022) consistently report negligible measurable Mg composition change under optimized LPBF parameters (weiss2022ontheinfluence pages 4-5, maamoun2018thermalpostprocessingof pages 3-5, smolina2022influenceofthe pages 8-11).

2. The Langmuir model predicts that Ar atmosphere at ~750–1000 mbar reduces evaporation rates by ~1000× relative to vacuum, making the absolute Mg loss per melt pool pass very small (bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 8-10).

3. At 0.05–0.07 wt% Mg, the Mg activity is 5–8× lower than in standard AlSi10Mg (0.3–0.4 wt%), further reducing the evaporative driving force (mukherjee2016printabilityofalloys pages 4-6, bartl2022influenceofsurface pages 8-10).

4. The dominant Mg loss mechanism in practice may be surface oxide formation (MgAl₂O₄ spinel) on spatter particles rather than bulk melt pool evaporation, as demonstrated by Raza et al. (2021) who observed progressive Mg enrichment in oxide nodules on AlSi10Mg spatter particles (raza2021degradationofalsi10mg pages 4-6, raza2021degradationofalsi10mg pages 6-8).

**Uncertainties and caveats:** (i) Aggressive process parameters (very high VED, slow scan speeds, keyhole-mode melting) could significantly increase Mg loss. (ii) Multiple remelting passes or remelting strategies increase total Mg evaporation, as shown for DED of AlMgScZr where remelted layers had lower Mg than un-remelted layers (zhao2024directedenergydeposition pages 65-70). (iii) The EDS/XRF measurement uncertainty (~0.02–0.05 wt% for Mg at these low levels) limits the ability to resolve small changes, so ICP-OES on digested bulk samples would be the recommended measurement approach for detecting losses of 0.005–0.01 wt% Mg. (iv) The already-low Mg content of the powder (0.05–0.07 wt%) is well below the AlSi10Mg specification minimum (0.20 wt%), meaning that even without any evaporative loss, the Mg₂Si age-hardening response will be severely limited regardless.

**Summary:** For a 0.05–0.07 wt% Mg AlSi10Mg powder processed under standard LPBF conditions in Ar, the predicted as-built Mg content is approximately **0.045–0.070 wt%**, with the most likely outcome being near-complete retention of the already-low Mg content. Any losses are expected to be ≤0.01 wt% in absolute terms, which is at or below the typical analytical uncertainty of EDS and comparable to the detection limits even for ICP-OES at these concentrations.
