Question: Is electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) routinely performed on metal powders, such as gas-atomized, water-atomized, plasma- or ultrasonically atomized powders used for additive manufacturing (especially aluminum alloys such as Al-Si, AlSi10Mg, 4047, 6061/6063)? How are powder particles prepared for EBSD (conductive or epoxy mounting and cross-section polishing, vibratory or colloidal-silica final polishing, broad ion beam milling, FIB cross-sections, as-received particle surfaces), and what per-particle information does EBSD provide (number of grains per particle, grain size versus particle size, dendrite/cell orientation, phase identification and spatial distribution of eutectic Si, orientation relationships, misorientation/deformation from atomization or satellites)? When is powder X-ray diffraction preferred or complementary instead (phase identification, phase fractions by Rietveld refinement, aluminum lattice parameter as a measure of Si in supersaturated solid solution, crystallite size, microstrain/residual stress), and what are each technique's detection limits and sampling statistics (number of particles probed)? Give concrete examples with citations and DOIs.

## Bottom line

**Yes—EBSD is an established research method for *individual metal-powder particles*, including gas-atomized AlSi10Mg and Al 6061. It is not, on the evidence reviewed, a routine lot-by-lot powder-quality test.** Its value is answering *which grains and phases occur in which particle and where*; mounting, exceptionally good surface preparation, mapping time, and limited particle counts make bulk powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) more practical for population-average crystalline phase and lattice measurements. An EBSD image of a *printed part made from atomized powder* is not evidence that EBSD was performed on the starting powder. (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 3-5, sousa2022microstructuralandmicromechanical pages 5-7, opatova2020relationshipbetweenthe pages 3-6)

### Demonstrated powder-particle examples

- **AlSi10Mg and AlSi10Mg–Cu, gas atomization.** Vanzetti *et al.* mapped cross-sections of 20–50 µm powders containing 0, 4, 8, or 20 wt% added Cu. Their EBSD band-contrast, Al-grain, and phase maps show **multiple secondary dendrite arms within one crystallographic grain**, and locate θ-Al₂Cu in interdendritic channels, preferentially near grain boundaries. Al grains became finer as Cu increased. For comparison, measured secondary-dendrite-arm spacings—not EBSD grain diameters—were **2.19 ± 0.56, 1.33 ± 0.36, 0.98 ± 0.23, and 0.69 ± 0.15 µm**, respectively. Their Figure 10 shows the powder EBSD maps; Figure 11 shows the grain-size distributions. DOI: **10.3390/met13111845**. (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 10-14, vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 7-10, vanzetti2023designandcharacterization media b4109c19, vanzetti2023designandcharacterization media 1a1c944b)
- **AlSi10Mg, two commercial gas-atomized batches.** Albu *et al.* prepared actual powder cross-sections for SEM/EBSD and separately studied printed specimens. Correlative STEM, rather than conventional EBSD alone, established that the powder’s eutectic Si network includes **5–30 nm crystalline Si and amorphous/disordered Si**; some Si particles within α-Al were **5–10 nm**. Thus an apparent cell network is not automatically a map of separate EBSD-indexable grains or phases. DOI: **10.1016/j.addma.2020.101605**. (albu2020microstructureevolutionduring pages 2-3, albu2020microstructureevolutionduring pages 3-4)
- **AA6061, gas-atomized cold-spray feedstock.** Sabard and Hussain mapped **powder particles before spraying**, as well as the subsequently deposited coatings. As-atomized particles had small, irregular or elongated grains, low-angle boundaries, and dendritic segregation; solution-treated particles showed larger, more equiaxed grains and fewer low-angle boundaries. Some individual grains contained several dendritic cells. The reported powder had **Dv10/Dv50/Dv90 = 24.9/36.5/53.0 µm**. Do not attribute the much stronger interparticle deformation mapped *after cold spraying* to the untouched feedstock. Published article DOI: **10.1007/s10853-019-03736-w**. (sabard2019interparticlebondingin pages 4-6, sabard2019interparticlebondingin pages 6-9, sabard2019interparticlebondingin pages 2-4)
- **Other production routes.** Skalon *et al.* used EBSD to distinguish polycrystalline **air-atomized commercially pure Al** from particles remelted and slowly resolidified with few crystals (DOI: **10.3390/met9030375**). EBSD also mapped α/β phase variation **among plasma-atomized Ti–6Al–4V powder particles** (Albu *et al.*, DOI: **10.1007/s00501-020-00958-3**). FIB–EBSD mapped internal structures of atomized maraging-steel powder (Opatová *et al.*, DOI: **10.3390/ma13040956**). These demonstrate that the approach is not restricted to gas atomization or Al alloys; they do **not** establish a universal grain structure for water-, plasma-, or ultrasonically atomized powders. In the literature examined, a defensible *powder-particle* EBSD case for **AA4047 or AA6063**, or an equally specific example for **ultrasonically atomized Al powder**, was not found. (skalon2019preparationmethodof pages 4-8, albu2020microstructureinvestigationsof pages 2-5, opatova2020relationshipbetweenthe pages 3-6)

### Preparing particles and interpreting their maps

**For a survey of many interiors, mount and polish a powder cross-section.** Vanzetti *et al.* embedded AlSi10Mg-based powder in conductive PolyFast resin, mechanically polished to 1 µm, then vibratory-polished for **2 h with 0.02 µm colloidal alumina**; EBSD used **150 nm steps** and a **10°** grain-boundary criterion. They etched *separate SEM specimens*, but left EBSD specimens as polished. Sabard and Hussain instead used **epoxy**, P1200 grinding, 6 and 1 µm diamond, and **0.06 µm colloidal-silica final polishing** for Al 6061. Albu *et al.* embedded AlSi10Mg in resin and used a final **plasma-ion polish**. A conductive mount assists charge control and electrical grounding; it does not replace removal of the deformed surface layer. DOI: **10.3390/met13111845; 10.1007/s10853-019-03736-w; 10.1016/j.addma.2020.101605**. (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 3-5, sabard2019interparticlebondingin pages 2-4, albu2020microstructureevolutionduring pages 2-3)

**Ion milling and FIB are alternatives, not mandatory stages of every powder EBSD workflow.** Broad Ar-ion cross-sectional polishing can expose many embedded particles without individually trenching each one; the feedstock-characterization review by Sousa *et al.* explicitly contrasts its throughput and cost with FIB preparation (DOI: **10.3390/powders1030010**). For targeted interiors or serial sections, Opatová *et al.* mounted particles on carbon adhesive, removed successive approximately **200 nm FIB slices**, and used a pre-tilted holder to acquire EDS/EBSD on cut faces. Their approximately **30 virgin plus 30 reused particles** were *FIB-sectioned*; this number is not a claim that 60 full EBSD maps or independent 3D grain reconstructions were obtained. DOI: **10.3390/ma13040956**. (sousa2022microstructuralandmicromechanical pages 5-7, opatova2020relationshipbetweenthe pages 3-6)

**As-received exterior surfaces answer a different question.** Loose powder on adhesive tape is useful for SEM surface morphology, satellites, and chemistry, but a curved, oxidized, rough surface varies in local tilt and may index poorly. A freshly polished planar section is the defensible default for comparing internal grain counts and phases; deliberately milling a selected particle can expose the relationship between a satellite and its parent. Apparent low-angle boundaries must also be checked against preparation artifacts: Skalon *et al.* explicitly observed intragrain misorientation associated with **hot mounting at 453 K**. DOI: **10.3390/met9030375**. (albu2020microstructureevolutionduring pages 2-3, skalon2019preparationmethodof pages 4-8, opatova2020relationshipbetweenthe pages 3-6)

For **each successfully indexed particle section**, EBSD can supply the *number of grains intersected by that two-dimensional section*, their sizes relative to that particle’s measured sectional diameter, orientations and boundary misorientations; correlated SEM/EDS reveals how dendritic or cellular solute segregation relates to those grains. Phase mapping can locate sufficiently large, crystalline, distinguishable constituents and establish orientation relationships **if both sides of an interface index reliably**. Maps of orientation spread or kernel-average misorientation can flag lattice rotations, including possible deformation near a satellite junction, but are **not a unique quantitative measure of atomization-induced dislocation density or stress**: solidification substructure, sectioning, polishing, and indexing also matter. One section cannot establish an exact *three-dimensional* grain count; serial FIB sections or 3D methods are required for that claim. (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 10-14, sabard2019interparticlebondingin pages 4-6, sousa2022microstructuralandmicromechanical pages 5-7, opatova2020relationshipbetweenthe pages 3-6)

**An important Al–Si failure mode:** Vanzetti *et al.* judged the plain AlSi10Mg EBSD phase/grain map **unreliable** because conventional Hough indexing confused fcc Al with diamond-cubic Si; one map indicated **21.8% Si** although the compositions should have had broadly similar Si contents. They restricted comparative *Al-grain* analysis to Cu-containing powders. For fine or partly amorphous eutectic Si, use correlative BSE/EDS, TEM/STEM diffraction or chemistry, and powder XRD rather than treating an EBSD color fraction as validated eutectic-Si content. Sabard and Hussain likewise could not confirm fine AA6061 precipitates with their EBSD step size. (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 10-14, albu2020microstructureevolutionduring pages 3-4, sabard2019interparticlebondingin pages 4-6)

### When powder XRD is preferable—and when it is not

**Choose XRD for an aliquot-average crystalline phase inventory, Rietveld phase fractions, or precise lattice constants.** On the *same gas-atomized AlSi10Mg–Cu powders* used for EBSD, Vanzetti *et al.* identified α-Al, crystalline Si, and θ-Al₂Cu and tracked increasing θ fraction with added Cu; **Q phase was not detected**, despite a model predicting roughly **1%** and TEM/EDS showing compositionally suggestive interdendritic precipitates. Their as-atomized α-Al parameter changed from **4.048 Å** without added Cu to **4.042 Å** with 8–20 wt% Cu, whereas after annealing it was approximately **4.050 Å** for every composition. This is evidence of retained solute/lattice changes, **not a Si-only calibration** in Cu-bearing alloy. DOI: **10.3390/met13111845**. (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 5-7, vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 10-14)

For gas-atomized AlSi10Mg without deliberate Cu addition, Albu *et al.* measured α-Al **a = 0.4046 nm** in powder A and **0.4050 nm** in powder B against their annealed reference **0.4051 nm**. A smaller Al lattice parameter is *consistent with* excess Si retained in α-Al, but composition, other solutes, vacancies/defects, temperature and calibration can affect the inference. Studies of rapidly solidified Al–Si products explicitly report that **Si and Cu both contract α-Al**, while Mg complicates Si estimates; a high-resolution Al–Si–Mg study found Si concentrations inferred using different lattice–composition models could differ substantially. Thus lattice parameter is a useful comparative *proxy*, not an independent assay of absolute dissolved Si without an alloy-appropriate standard or complementary chemistry. DOI: **10.1016/j.addma.2020.101605; 10.1016/j.jallcom.2019.153538; 10.1016/j.matchar.2021.110881**. The last two works measure melt-spun/printed material, **not starting-powder EBSD**. (albu2020microstructureevolutionduring pages 3-4, marola2020alloyingalsi10mgand pages 3-5, casati2021insightintothe pages 2-3)

**Peak breadth** can, with instrumental broadening calibrated and size/strain effects modeled, estimate coherent diffraction-domain (‘crystallite’) size and microstrain. Neither is the diameter of the powder particle; coherent domains need not equal EBSD grains. For example, high-resolution XRD estimated approximately **9–10 nm crystalline-Si domains** in *printed* AlSi7Mg—an illustration of what line-profile analysis measures, **not a measurement of atomized AlSi10Mg powder grain size**. Peak-position measurements at different specimen orientations can estimate residual elastic stress under appropriate geometry and diffraction-elastic assumptions; a line-width-derived microstrain is **not** synonymous with macroscopic residual stress. A freely poured powder bed has no single continuous part-scale stress field: interphase or intraparticle self-equilibrated stresses require particular care in interpretation. DOI: **10.1016/j.matchar.2021.110881; 10.1299/jsmea.47.252**. (casati2021insightintothe pages 2-3, sivasankaran2016structuralcharacterizationand pages 3-7, tanaka2004diffractionmeasurementsof pages 2-4)

**Detection limits are conditional, not fixed specifications.** Conventional EBSD’s often-quoted favorable spatial resolution is of order **20–50 nm**, but actual *phase* detectability also depends on interaction volume, scan step, phase dimensions and crystallinity, polish quality, phase-library selection and misindexing. A crystalline minority phase can be detected where it is deliberately mapped even if rare in the bulk; amorphous Si cannot yield a conventional crystalline EBSD pattern. For laboratory powder XRD, a **few wt%** is a prudent *order-of-magnitude expectation* for an uncomplicated minor phase, **not a universal limit or Rietveld uncertainty**: an isolated sharp peak can outperform that figure, whereas a broad or overlapping peak, texture or amorphous content can make even a more abundant constituent difficult to identify. Specifically, the ~1%-predicted Q phase in Vanzetti’s powders was not observed; absent Bragg peaks do **not** prove zero Q or zero amorphous Si. An internal standard or appropriate calibrated method is needed to quantify amorphous material rather than simply normalizing the detected crystalline phases to 100%. (carneiro2020recentadvancesin pages 1-2, albu2020microstructureevolutionduring pages 3-4, vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 10-14, aranda*2012rietveldquantitativephase pages 29-31, ali2022xraydiffractiontechniques pages 15-16)

**Sampling must be reported by method, not implied by the number of pixels.** An EBSD scan may contain millions of pixels but only a small number of *independent particles*; Vanzetti *et al.* report **ten particles and at least 100 measurements per alloy for SEM dendrite spacing**, **not** a verified EBSD particle count. Opatová *et al.* reported ~30 particles per powder history for targeted FIB slicing; their **130-particle** size-distribution measurements are a separate statistic. At the opposite extreme, Heiden *et al.* used powder XRD to estimate **0–1 wt% ferrite in virgin versus 1–2 wt% in reused 316L**, while cross-sectional EBSD revealed rare **5–50 µm fully ferritic single-crystal reused particles**. EBSD area fractions of **0.30% and 6.79%** in selected maps should not be equated to those powder-average **weight** fractions: the observed particles, mapped area, indexing and area-versus-mass weighting differ. DOI: **10.3390/met13111845; 10.3390/ma13040956; 10.1016/j.addma.2018.10.019**. (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 3-5, opatova2020relationshipbetweenthe pages 3-6, heiden2019evolutionof316l pages 8-10)

For scale, an *illustrative calculation*, **not a reported XRD count**: 0.1 g of fully illuminated, monodisperse **40 µm-diameter** Al spheres at density **2.7 g cm⁻³** contains about **1.1 million particles** [mass divided by density × πd³/6]. A conventional powder scan may actually illuminate only a subset of a holder at one instant, and absorption, packing, rotation, grain statistics and aliquot segregation matter. Even so, bulk XRD generally averages vastly more particles than a typical particle-resolved EBSD study, while **never telling how many grains were in any one of them**. Accordingly, use XRD for reproducible *ensemble* phase/lattice comparisons and EBSD on appropriately stratified particle cross-sections for *heterogeneity, grain structure and spatial relationships*; corroborate nanoscale eutectic Si and suspect phases with TEM/STEM and chemical imaging. (heiden2019evolutionof316l pages 3-6, vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 10-14, sousa2022microstructuralandmicromechanical pages 5-7, opatova2020relationshipbetweenthe pages 3-6)
