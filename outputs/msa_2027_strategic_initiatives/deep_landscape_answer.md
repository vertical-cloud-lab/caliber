Question: We are preparing a proposal to the Microscopy Society of America (MSA) 2027 Strategic Initiatives program for an MSA-branded, open-source platform (CALIBER) that couples retrieval-augmented generation over the microscopy and microanalysis literature with a growing experimental dataset and an uncertainty-aware feedback loop to recommend high-fidelity SEM, EDS, EBSD, and XRF acquisition parameters for novel alloys, and that collects, preserves, and disseminates parameter-selection knowledge (technical procedures, underlying physics, and the rationale behind settings recommended in standards). Produce a citation-backed landscape review covering: (1) existing open-source software and data standards in microscopy/microanalysis relevant to this effort (e.g., HyperSpy, kikuchipy, pyxem, EMsoft, DREAM.3D, ImageJ/Fiji, napari, OME-Zarr, NeXus/FAIRmat) and what niches they do NOT cover; (2) existing repositories and resources for microscopy technical procedures and protocols (e.g., protocols.io, MyScope, DoITPoMS, nanoHUB, Microscopy Today and Microscopy & Microanalysis archives) and their gaps in capturing and preserving the rationale behind acquisition parameter choices; (3) evidence on FAIR-data and metadata-capture gaps in electron microscopy and microanalysis workflows; (4) precedents where scientific societies built branded open digital resources or tools, and what made them succeed or fail; (5) prior work on machine learning, literature mining, or autonomous/self-driving microscopy approaches to instrument parameter recommendation, and how an uncertainty-aware, literature-grounded recommender would differ; (6) successful models for involving students and early-career scientists in curating community scientific knowledge bases. Conclude with the strongest gap statements such a proposal could cite.

# Landscape Review for the CALIBER Proposal: Citation-Backed Gap Analysis for an MSA-Branded, Open-Source Microscopy Knowledge Platform

## 1. Open-Source Software and Data Standards in Microscopy/Microanalysis

The open-source microscopy ecosystem is rich in complementary tools for post-acquisition analysis, simulation, reconstruction, visualization, and data storage, yet no existing resource provides unified, literature-grounded acquisition-parameter recommendation.

**HyperSpy** serves as a foundational Python library for exploring, visualizing, and analyzing multidimensional microscopy and spectroscopy data, with lazy Dask-based processing that scales from desktops to high-performance computing (wei2022infrastructureforanalysis pages 1-3, pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 21-24). **Pyxem** and **py4DSTEM** extend this ecosystem for electron-diffraction and 4D-STEM analysis respectively (wei2022infrastructureforanalysis pages 1-3). **Kikuchipy** provides open-source EBSD pattern processing and indexing with interoperability to HyperSpy and EMsoft-generated simulated patterns. **EMsoft** is a physics-based forward-simulation suite for dynamical electron scattering and EBSD master-pattern generation; it is widely used for dictionary indexing and ML training but requires users to formulate candidate conditions rather than recommending them. **DREAM.3D** operates downstream of acquisition for 3D microstructure reconstruction, segmentation, and synthetic-volume generation. **Pycroscopy/pyUSID** provide a standardized USID/HDF5 data model linking source data, metadata, and derived results for improved traceability and reproducibility (somnath2019usidandpycroscopy pages 12-16, somnath2019usidandpycroscopy pages 19-23). **AtomAI** offers deep-learning analysis for microscopy images and spectra, including segmentation and structure–property mapping (pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 21-24). **ImageJ/Fiji** and **napari** serve as general-purpose image analysis and visualization platforms respectively.

For data standards, **OME-Zarr** provides cloud-native, chunked, multiscale array storage, but its semantics are predominantly oriented toward biological optical microscopy rather than materials microanalysis (taillon2025mardafairmaterials pages 3-4). **NeXus/FAIRmat NXem** offers a hierarchical, schema-validated representation of electron-microscopy experiments, covering instruments, samples, acquisition settings, and provenance for SEM, TEM, and STEM workflows (donni2025fairdatamanagementa pages 13-17, donni2025fairdatamanagement pages 13-17). However, even NXem has reported schema gaps for fields such as magnification, working distance, pixel size, and stage tilt in certain implementations (balestra2025fairdatamanagement pages 40-47).

**Critically, none of these tools provides a unified system that couples literature/standards retrieval with structured experimental data, multi-modal SEM/EDS/EBSD/XRF parameter recommendation, calibrated uncertainty, closed-loop learning, and preservation of procedural and decision rationale** (pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 5-7, pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 1-3, pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 3-5). The broader ecosystem remains fragmented, with limited benchmarks, integration strategies, and upstream experiment-planning capabilities (kuhbach2022communitydrivenmethodsfor pages 1-2, pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 5-7).

## 2. Repositories for Microscopy Technical Procedures and Protocols

Existing protocol repositories and educational resources—including protocols.io, MyScope, DoITPoMS, nanoHUB, and the archives of *Microscopy Today* and *Microscopy & Microanalysis*—generally document the procedural steps of experiments but fail to systematically capture the expert reasoning behind parameter choices. Conventional publications compress acquisition, analysis, and post-processing information into short methods paragraphs, leaving inadequate space for the full chain of decisions needed to reproduce measurements (gammon2023anonlinerepository pages 1-2, gammon2023anonlinerepository pages 2-3). As Gammon et al. (2023) note, published settings are starting points that expert users must modify for their own instruments and specimens, yet the rationale for these modifications is rarely recorded (gammon2023anonlinerepository pages 2-3).

In electron microscopy specifically, effective experiments depend on both instrument-specific expertise and broader physical/chemical knowledge, including judgments about what is important to observe and what acquisition strategies to pursue (kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 4-7). Acquisition decisions are sequential: regions may be rescanned, spectroscopy may be initiated, or the sample may be modified based on morphology, spectral response, prior knowledge, or novelty. Repositories need to represent contextual priors, hypotheses, and the interaction between human decisions and ML/AI systems—not merely final parameters (kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 4-7). Current workflows are often custom, multistep processes refined through operator intuition over weeks, making expert knowledge extremely difficult to transfer or reproduce across laboratories (pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 3-5). The reasoning behind parameter choices remains implicit rather than systematically recorded, and repositories may document what settings were used without capturing why they were selected, what alternatives were considered, or how observations during an experiment changed subsequent decisions (pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 3-5, kuhbach2022communitydrivenmethodsfor pages 2-3).

## 3. FAIR-Data and Metadata-Capture Gaps in Electron Microscopy and Microanalysis

The materials microscopy community routinely fails to capture the complete experimental context needed to make data findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable. The MaRDA Working Group on FAIR Materials Microscopy Metadata recommends that instruments capture comprehensive metadata about operators, specimens/samples, instrument conditions, and data formation, and that microscopy data use standardized vocabularies and community-standard identifiers—recommendations that reflect the current inadequacy of practice (taillon2025mardafairmaterials pages 4-5, taillon2025mardafairmaterials pages 3-4). Commonly omitted metadata includes: unambiguous sample descriptions and persistent identifiers; microscope and detector identity; user identity; sample-preparation details; instrument settings and environmental conditions; deviations from standard procedures; and calibration procedures and results (taillon2025mardafairmaterials pages 4-5).

For SEM specifically, metadata at risk of being lost includes accelerating voltage, magnification, working distance, probe current, filament current, emitter type, detector information, microscope model and vendor, and control-software version—information that may be stored in proprietary structures (e.g., CZ_SEM EXIF tags) that are difficult to read automatically and are not guaranteed to survive format conversion (donni2025fairdatamanagementa pages 38-41, donni2025fairdatamanagement pages 38-41). Commercial instruments may produce proprietary formats, and vendor software can discard substantial portions of instrument-condition metadata when exporting because standard formats do not accommodate vendor- and instrument-specific fields (ghiringhelli2023sharedmetadatafor pages 13-14). The Shared Metadata for Data-Centric Materials Science initiative further highlights that experimental materials are often incompletely characterized and that environmental conditions such as temperature, air pressure, and humidity are commonly absent from records (ghiringhelli2023sharedmetadatafor pages 13-14).

A particularly significant finding is that FAIR metadata standards can structure and preserve acquisition context, but they do not by themselves capture the *causal rationale* for parameter selection—why a particular voltage was chosen, what the expected trade-offs were, or what standards clause justified the decision (donni2025fairdatamanagementa pages 13-17, donni2025fairdatamanagement pages 13-17). This represents a fundamental gap that CALIBER is designed to fill.

## 4. Precedents for Society-Branded Open Digital Resources

Multiple precedents inform how scientific societies and community organizations have successfully built branded open digital infrastructure. The **Computational Infrastructure for Geodynamics (CIG)** succeeded by combining a clear disciplinary mission with dissemination of open-source software, training, and enforceable practices such as open licenses, version control, and automated testing that eventually became community standards (katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 12-14). **NumFOCUS** built an effective umbrella model through fiscal sponsorship, educational programs, and support for widely used open-source projects. **rOpenSci** paired branded technical infrastructure—vetted, reusable R packages—with social infrastructure including peer review, communication channels, contributor support, and in-person events that built trust and sustained participation. **MolSSI** similarly combined software engineering, standards work, mentoring, and fellowships (katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 12-14).

In the life sciences, **ELIXIR** coordinates databases, software tools, training materials, and computational resources across an intergovernmental network, with 4OSS recommendations, benchmarking frameworks, and registries illustrating a branded ecosystem of open, discoverable, and reusable digital resources (katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 8-10). The **Science Gateways Community Institute (SGCI)** provides another model through catalogs, developer support, incubators, bootcamps, and student programs (katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 8-10). In scholarly publishing, **BioOne** created an open-access partnership among scientific societies, academic institutions, and the commercial sector supported by nearly 900 libraries (schweik2007freeopensourcesoftwareas pages 111-115), while the American Anthropological Association's **AnthroSource** portal was developed to provide members with broad online access while retaining society ownership and control of content (schweik2007freeopensourcesoftwareas pages 108-111).

**Key success factors** across these precedents include: community ownership and governance; concrete services that solve real needs; sustained maintenance and dedicated staff; training and capacity building; contributor recognition; integration with existing community activities rather than competing with them; and diversified financial support (katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 12-14, katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 14-16). **Failure risks** include volunteer commitments that exceed available capacity, initiatives that lack an established operational base, and attempts to launch entirely new activities without alignment to existing infrastructure. The WSSSPE experience illustrates this: working groups were more successful when aligned with existing activities, while attempts to create large independent programs generally failed (katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 12-14).

## 5. Machine Learning, Literature Mining, and Autonomous Microscopy Approaches

Substantial prior work exists on automated and autonomous experimentation in microscopy. Kalinin et al. (2021) provide a comprehensive framework distinguishing automated experiments—where AI performs rapid low-level analysis—from autonomous experimentation, where systems can seek additional information and adapt experimental progression (kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 7-10, kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 4-7). Key ML paradigms include **Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization** for recommending instrument parameters through acquisition functions that balance predicted performance and uncertainty; **reinforcement learning** for sequential instrument control; and **active learning** for targeting unusual or informative regions for additional measurements (kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 10-13, kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 22-25).

For SEM specifically, existing work has progressed from image-sharpness-based autofocus to deep-learning systems that evaluate focus quality and control working distance, beam-profile deconvolution methods that independently optimize focus and stigmator settings, and reinforcement learning for real-time imaging-parameter optimization (abbasgholinejadasbaghi2025metadatadrivenoptimizationin pages 12-15). A metadata-driven SEM optimization approach proposes automated recommendation of accelerating voltage, working distance, and aperture size using polynomial regression, metadata clustering, and iterative learning evaluated through no-reference image quality metrics (abbasgholinejadasbaghi2025metadatadrivenoptimizationin pages 10-12, abbasgholinejadasbaghi2025metadatadrivenoptimizationin pages 7-10).

**However, existing autonomous microscopy systems share critical limitations that CALIBER would address.** Current systems are primarily task-specific and data-driven: they identify predefined objects or regions (e.g., atoms, grain boundaries, domain walls) and trigger measurements according to fixed policies (pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 3-5). They do not synthesize domain literature and standards, they lack mechanisms for tracing recommendations to specific standards clauses or published evidence, and they do not learn from accumulating community experiments across heterogeneous instruments (pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 3-5, kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 18-22). An uncertainty-aware, literature-grounded recommender would differ by: explicitly encoding and exposing scientific priors and standards rationale; retrieving external knowledge during operation through RAG over the microscopy literature; quantifying and distinguishing aleatoric, epistemic, and out-of-distribution uncertainty; using that uncertainty to abstain or recommend informative follow-up measurements; detecting distribution shift across instruments and materials; and preserving human oversight and interpretability (kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 10-13, kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 7-10, kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 18-22). Kalinin et al. (2021) specifically identify the absence of a general framework balancing human and ML/AI decision-making with instrumental considerations as a key gap (kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 4-7).

## 6. Models for Involving Students and Early-Career Scientists

Multiple successful models demonstrate how trainees can contribute to community knowledge curation. **Software Carpentry** recruits researchers as volunteer peer instructors, trains them in evidence-based pedagogy, and enables them to teach reproducible workflows—creating a distributed model in which participants become both learners and contributors through collaborative lesson development, local Champions, and a Community Cookbook (katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 10-12). The broader Mozilla model similarly emphasizes cohort-based training, mentorship, mini-grants, and graduates returning as mentors (katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 10-12).

**Hackathons** provide focused collaborative events. The Mic-hackathon 2024 brought together ML experts and microscopy professionals to develop benchmark datasets, digital twins, and analysis tools for electron and scanning probe microscopy (pratiush2025michackathon2024hackathon pages 1-3). NASA's International Space Apps Challenge engaged more than 15,000 participants from 150 countries (ramachandran2021fromopendata pages 8-8). **Wiki-based curation** through PLOS Computational Biology's Topic Pages model allows students and early-career researchers to write peer-reviewed articles that are simultaneously published in the journal and on Wikipedia (arbuckle2021creatingopenscholarship pages 36-38, arbuckle2021creatingopenscholarship pages 33-36).

**Structured student projects** offer another model: Toelch and Ostwald (2018) describe MSc students converting existing datasets into the Brain Imaging Data Structure (BIDS), reproducing analyses with readable code, and contributing to data-descriptor publications—developing data-management and reproducibility skills while producing reusable community resources (toelch2018digitalopenscience—teaching pages 5-6). Rainford et al. (2026) recommend targeted training programs, coding groups, peer-mentoring schemes, and AI-assisted documentation tools such as PRISM, where researchers record protocols hands-free and AI generates annotated video and text documentation (rainford2026knowledgepreservationin pages 6-7).

**Critical design principles** for sustained participation include: bounded, meaningful tasks with clear onboarding; structured mentorship and peer review; visible credit and recognition for contributions; version control so resources remain living records; accessible collaborative infrastructure; and a progression from participant to contributor to leader and community advocate (rainford2026knowledgepreservationin pages 6-7, katz2018communityorganizationschanging pages 10-12). One-off crowdsourcing without scaffolding or recognition is unlikely to maintain quality or participation (arbuckle2021creatingopenscholarship pages 38-41, arbuckle2021creatingopenscholarship pages 33-36).

### Overarching Gap Synthesis

Across all six domains, the evidence converges on a central finding: **the microscopy and microanalysis community possesses a maturing ecosystem of post-acquisition analysis tools and emerging metadata standards, but it lacks connective infrastructure that translates the community's accumulated literature, standards, and experimental experience into actionable, uncertainty-aware acquisition guidance while preserving the expert rationale that currently exists only as tacit knowledge** (kalinin2021automatedandautonomous pages 4-7, rainford2026knowledgepreservationin pages 2-3, rainford2026knowledgepreservationin pages 1-2).

Science loses knowledge when researchers retire or move between roles because much of the practical expertise behind experiments—troubleshooting experience, contextual understanding, and the reasoning behind methodological choices—is not captured in publications or records (rainford2026knowledgepreservationin pages 6-7, rainford2026knowledgepreservationin pages 2-3, rainford2026knowledgepreservationin pages 1-2). In microscopy specifically, preserving the rationale behind experimental decisions requires recording adaptations, controls, validation, lessons learned, and contextual explanations—not merely final data or protocols (rainford2026knowledgepreservationin pages 6-7, kalinin2608beyondfairdata pages 1-3). Kalinin et al. propose "Instrument Traces" that capture not only successful measurements but also exploratory paths, decision provenance, and motivations, making expertise transferable and supporting operator training and reproducible autonomy (kalinin2608beyondfairdata pages 1-3).

CALIBER would fill this gap by coupling retrieval-augmented generation over the microscopy literature with a growing experimental dataset and an uncertainty-aware feedback loop, creating a platform that not only recommends high-fidelity acquisition parameters but also collects, preserves, and disseminates the parameter-selection knowledge—technical procedures, underlying physics, and the rationale behind settings recommended in standards—that the community currently risks losing with each generation of retiring microscopists.
