Question: Provide quantitative, citable evidence on how acquisition parameter choices determine data fidelity in SEM-based microanalysis of metal alloys, suitable for the needs statement of a funding proposal. Cover: (1) standardless SEM-EDS quantification error and its dependence on accelerating voltage, beam current/dead time, take-off geometry, and standards-based vs standardless mode, including documented cases of large errors for minor/trace elements (e.g., Mg in AlSi10Mg or similar Al alloys); (2) EBSD indexing success rate and confidence index dependence on beam current, accelerating voltage, pattern exposure/binning, and phase-list selection, especially for additively manufactured (laser powder bed fusion) Al-Si alloys; (3) micro-XRF quantification sensitivity to tube voltage, current, filters, and matrix corrections for metal alloys; (4) published evidence that acquisition parameter selection remains trial-and-error and expertise-dependent, and that the rationale behind recommended settings in standards (e.g., ASTM E1508, ISO 22309) and vendor guides is sparsely documented or hard to access. # Quantitative evidence for a funding-proposal needs statement

## Executive conclusion

Acquisition settings are not merely operational details: they determine whether SEM-EDS returns approximately quantitative chemistry or a normalized but biased composition, whether EBSD maps contain mostly correct orientations or mostly failed/misassigned pixels, and whether micro-XRF detects minor alloying additions or overestimates them by orders of magnitude. The literature supports a strong need for **alloy-, instrument-, and task-specific acquisition-response models**, especially for LPBF Al-Si-Mg, because controlled factorial studies connecting settings to ground-truth fidelity are sparse.

## 1. SEM-EDS quantification

### Standards-based versus standardless accuracy

An NPL uncertainty assessment states that fully quantitative SEM-EDS under stringent conditions and with physical standards can achieve approximately **±5% relative uncertainty**, whereas standardless methods can have uncertainty as high as **±30%**. Normalized standardless analysis is particularly hazardous because forcing the reported total to 100 wt% can hide missing elements or large errors behind a plausible-looking result (tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 1-8).

For a NIST Fe-Cr-Ni reference alloy, normalized standardless analysis at **20 kV** gave errors of **+1.3% Cr, −0.4% Fe, and −0.8% Ni** on one system. A second system at **25 kV** gave **−3.1% Cr, +1.3% Fe, and −4.1% Ni**. The investigators could not determine why the systems differed because detector hardware, analysis algorithms, geometry calibration, peak fitting, matrix corrections, and remote standards databases were manufacturer-constrained (tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 18-21).

Unnormalized standardless analysis of the same alloy gave relative errors of **−0.7% Cr, −2.3% Fe, and −2.7% Ni**, with a visibly deficient total of **98.1 wt%**. Physical pure-element standards reduced errors to **<1% relative to NIST EDS values**, although residual disagreement with wet chemistry remained **3–7%**, attributed to ZAF matrix corrections. Standards measured in the same session cancel much of the dependence on beam current, detector geometry, contamination, and detector efficiency (tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 21-24).

### Accelerating voltage

ISO 22309 specifies an electron overvoltage of at least **1.8**, meaning incident energy should be at least 1.8 times the energy of every X-ray line used quantitatively. For example, adequate Fe-K excitation requires at least approximately **12 kV**. Voltage therefore cannot be selected solely for spatial resolution: it determines whether a line is efficiently excited and also changes interaction volume and absorption (tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 8-11).

The dependence is experimentally visible. Standardless measurements of the same light-element material at **5, 10, and 20 kV** produced large between-voltage scatter; pooled results had errors of **−19% C, −31% N, +2.8% O, +15% F, and +3.4% S**. Although this is not an alloy, it demonstrates the pronounced voltage sensitivity of low-energy lines relevant to Mg and Al analysis (tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 18-21).

### Beam current, count rate, and dead time

Beam current increases X-ray counts and reduces counting uncertainty, but high input rates increase dead time, pulse pile-up, and sum peaks. One parameter-optimization study identified beam current/intensity and dead time as the variables exerting the greatest influence on EDS results and recommended operating at **10–20% dead time** (kallio2025accuracyandprecision pages 59-62).

For rigorous standards-based analysis, the NPL reference-alloy experiment limited measured beam-current drift to **0.2%** over a **20 min** acquisition session. Unnormalized standardless analysis likewise requires a stable current because a separately acquired reference spectrum normalizes current and detector geometry. Ratio-based methods that derive elemental ratios within the same spectrum can suppress current instability and have reported accuracy better than **5%**, but their calibration factors remain beam-energy dependent (teng2020highresolutionxray pages 44-49, tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 21-24).

### Take-off geometry and specimen topography

The quantitative protocol requires a flat, polished surface normal to the beam and approximately **35° to the EDS detector**. In practical tests, holes and grooves substantially changed integrated counts and even the number of elements identified because low local take-off angles obstructed emitted X-rays (kallio2025accuracyandprecision pages 59-62, tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 18-21). This is directly relevant to LPBF alloys: pores, melt-pool relief, incompletely polished Si particles, and tilted surfaces preferentially absorb low-energy Mg and Al X-rays.

### Minor/trace Mg in aluminum

The retrieved literature did **not** provide a controlled SEM-EDS parameter sweep for Mg in LPBF AlSi10Mg against certified bulk chemistry. That absence is itself an important proposal gap. The strongest defensible SEM-EDS statement is that standardless errors can reach **±30%**, light-element uncertainty is greater, and normalized output can conceal missing mass (tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 1-8, tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 21-24).

Closely analogous XRF alloy evidence shows how severe the Al-matrix/light-element problem can become: Mg in AlMg4.5Mn0.7 was over-reported by approximately **2 wt%**, or **40% relative**, while nominal Al99.5 was reported as **0.196 wt% Mg instead of 0.004 wt%**, a **5000% relative deviation**. These are XRF rather than SEM-EDS results and must not be presented as SEM-EDS performance, but they quantitatively demonstrate why trace Mg in an Al matrix requires matrix-matched validation rather than reliance on an automatic alloy mode (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 10-13, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 8-10).

## 2. EBSD indexing and confidence

### Beam current and exposure

Beam current and exposure jointly determine electron dose and pattern signal-to-noise ratio. Direct current sweeps were not recovered for LPBF AlSi10Mg, but controlled frame-averaging data quantify the dose/throughput tradeoff. A study varied averaging among **1, 5, 10, 20, and 30 frames** over **3,848 patterns** in a duplex alloy. At one frame, essentially all patterns were misclassified; five improved the result, ten produced a reasonable fraction of correct classifications, and 20–30 produced nearly complete agreement with the reference phase map. Reducing averaging from 30 to five made acquisition **six times faster** while retaining high accuracy for most tested phases, but the most crystallographically similar phases remained vulnerable (kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 4-5, kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 5-8, kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 8-10).

Independent conventional-indexing data show the consequence of faster acquisition. Increasing collection rate from **54 to 154 patterns/s** reduced correctly indexed patterns from **34.9% to 9.3%**. At 54 patterns/s, rescanning or neighbor-based processing increased success to **41.3–59.7%**; at 154 patterns/s, improved processing recovered only approximately **12.5–37.6%**. Thus, throughput choices can change the usable fraction by tens of percentage points and make the final result strongly dependent on cleanup (wright2015introductionandcomparison pages 17-27).

### Accelerating voltage

A controlled study varied **10, 20, and 30 kV**, with 20 kV as the training/default condition. At 10 kV many patterns were misclassified; 30 kV produced reasonably good classifications. Lower voltage widened Kikuchi bands and changed their apparent spacing, yielding one of the two parameter changes that caused broadly noteworthy performance losses (the other was one-frame acquisition) (kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 5-8, kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 8-10).

For aluminum specifically, shot-peened 7075-T651 was examined at **5, 10, and 20 kV**, with **4×4 and 8×8 binning**. The study found dictionary indexing more robust than conventional Hough indexing for low-quality, low-voltage patterns, but did not report a complete numerical success-rate table for the acquisition sweep. This supports transferability to Al but also illustrates the scarcity of quantitative acquisition-response curves (singh2018highresolutionlow pages 6-6).

### Pattern exposure and binning/resolution

Pattern resolution was varied from **156×128** to **622×512** and **1244×1024 pixels**. Low-resolution acquisition retained high classification accuracy for most tested phases, but phases having similar structures were appreciably more sensitive. Thus, binning can accelerate mapping without a universal penalty, but its acceptability depends on the phase discrimination task (kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 5-8, kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 8-10).

Commercial image-quality metrics should not be treated as instrument-independent confidence measures. Published analysis notes that vendors use different definitions of band contrast, pattern quality, and image quality; at least one definition was not disclosed, and the available definitions were only partly documented. Beam current, accelerating voltage, working distance, charging, holder stability, surface condition, orientation, and crystal perfection all affect these metrics, yet their relative contributions, reproducibility, and strain sensitivity had not been quantitatively characterized (cocle2008developmentofthe pages 26-31, cocle2008developmentofthe pages 31-34).

### Phase-list selection

Phase-list choice can dominate otherwise ideal acquisition. For duplex steel, supplying the two correct phases produced a high-fidelity map. Supplying **12 candidate phases**—the two correct solutions plus ten crystallographically plausible alternatives—caused **not a single pattern** to index to either correct phase, even though the patterns were collected using the study’s ideal settings (kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 3-4, kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 4-5). This is highly relevant to LPBF Al-Si: adding α-Al, diamond-cubic Si, oxides, Mg₂Si, or metastable candidates without chemistry-informed constraints can create confident but false assignments.

### LPBF Al-Si evidence gap

The search recovered many EBSD applications to additively manufactured Al alloys, but not a controlled LPBF AlSi10Mg factorial study reporting indexing rate or confidence index against beam current, voltage, exposure, binning, and phase-list composition. Consequently, commonly published AlSi10Mg settings are examples rather than validated optima. This gap is a compelling funding need: LPBF Al-Si combines fine cellular α-Al/Si structure, high defect density, residual strain, pores, oxides, and polishing sensitivity, so generic EBSD defaults cannot be assumed to preserve phase and orientation fidelity.

## 3. Micro-XRF

### Tube voltage, current, and filters

A commercial portable-XRF alloy protocol used **15–50 kV** and **12–106 µA**, depending on the alloy class and concentration range. Automatic primary-beam filters included **75 µm Cu/25 µm Ti/200 µm Al**, **38 µm Al**, **25 µm Ti/300 µm Al**, and **25 µm Fe**. A **60 s** acquisition was divided into separate **30 s light-element and heavy-element scans** (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 4-5). These choices matter because tube voltage determines which absorption edges are excited, current controls incident flux, and filters trade continuum background against analyte-line intensity.

A micro-XRF component study operated at **40 kV/0.2 mA**, increased current from **0.2 to 1.0 mA** on an Ag-Cu alloy to test input-rate effects, and compared **12.5, 25, and 50 µm Mo** filters and **50 and 200 µm Al** filters. Polycapillary spectra were **35–40 times** more intense than monocapillary spectra; one optic provided an approximately **300-fold flux-density gain**, showing that sensitivity cannot be inferred from tube power alone (bichlmeier2001componentselectionfor pages 3-4, bichlmeier2001componentselectionfor pages 4-5).

At **40 kV, 0.2 mA, and 1000 s**, reported micro-XRF minimum detection limits were typically **10–100 ppm**; examples were **23–68 ppm Mn**, **10–22 ppm Rb**, and **68–94 ppm Ca**, depending on tube and optics. These values are not universal alloy limits because atmosphere, optic transmission, detector window, filter, matrix, and live time all differ (bichlmeier2001componentselectionfor pages 3-4, bichlmeier2001componentselectionfor pages 4-5).

### Matrix corrections and calibration

Fundamental-parameter correction does not eliminate matrix dependence. In a quantitative micro-XRF assessment, matrix-matched calibration reduced typical errors to **<5% for most major elements** and **<6% for some minor elements**. Without suitable standards, typical errors were below **50% for many major/minor elements** and reached **100% for trace elements**, with some relative errors exceeding 100% (flude2017applicationofbenchtop pages 21-23).

Metal-alloy measurements reinforce this point. Commercial EDXRF/WDXRF generally achieved absolute deviations below **1 wt% for matrix elements** and below **0.25 wt% for minor elements under 2 wt%**, but relative deviations above 100% occurred below 1 wt%. Mg in Al alloys produced the particularly large **40%** and **5000%** errors described above. The authors attributed the poor light-element performance to low Mg/Al/Si X-ray production cross-sections and strong absorption by air and the detector window (seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 10-13, seidel2021comparisonofelemental pages 8-10).

## 4. Why current practice remains expertise-dependent

The evidence supports a careful formulation: published practice is often **empirical, instrument-specific, and operator-mediated**, although the retrieved papers do not literally label every optimization procedure “trial-and-error.”

* The operator remains responsible for recognizing pile-up/escape artefacts, checking peak labels, and rejecting poor background fits; automated software does not remove this responsibility (tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 8-11).
* EBSD operating values are explicitly described as non-fixed and valid over manufacturer-dependent ranges. Accurate Hough phase mapping presupposes that the operator already knows and supplies the correct phases; a broader but plausible list can yield zero correct phase assignments (kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 3-4, kaufmann2021anacquisitionparameter pages 4-5).
* Commercial EBSD quality metrics are vendor-specific, partly documented, and in at least one case not disclosed. The relative effects of current, voltage, surface condition, orientation, and other variables remain unquantified (cocle2008developmentofthe pages 26-31, cocle2008developmentofthe pages 31-34).
* Investigators comparing standardless EDS systems could not identify the cause of different accuracies because relevant hardware and analysis methods were manufacturer-constrained (tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 18-21).

ASTM E1508 and ISO 22309 are therefore best characterized as supplying **minimum practices and operating constraints**, not transparent alloy-specific optimization models. The accessible evidence documents recommendations such as **overvoltage ≥1.8**, flat/polished geometry, and approximately **35° detector take-off**, but the retrieved literature does not provide the derivation, uncertainty surface, or validation data needed to translate those thresholds into optimal settings for a particular detector, alloy, microstructure, and analytical objective (tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 8-11, tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 18-21, tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 21-24). Claims that the standards contain “no rationale” would be too strong; the defensible proposal claim is that the rationale and quantitative response surfaces needed for specimen-specific optimization are **sparsely documented or not readily accessible**, particularly where vendor algorithms and calibration databases are proprietary.

## Proposal-ready needs statement

> SEM-based microanalysis lacks validated, transferable acquisition-response models linking controllable settings to ground-truth chemical and crystallographic fidelity in technologically important metal alloys. Standardless SEM-EDS may carry up to ±30% relative uncertainty, while standards-based analysis can approach ±5%; geometry, overvoltage, current, and dead time introduce additional coupled errors. EBSD acquisition speed alone has changed correct indexing from 34.9% to 9.3%, one-frame acquisition can misclassify nearly an entire map, and an over-inclusive phase list has produced zero correct phase assignments despite ideal patterns. In XRF alloy analysis, minor Mg has been overestimated by 40% and trace Mg by 5000%, while matrix-matched calibration can reduce major-element errors below 5%. Yet controlled, ground-truthed parameter sweeps are notably absent for LPBF AlSi10Mg. Standards and vendor guidance provide operating thresholds and defaults, but not transparent, alloy-specific uncertainty surfaces. Funding is therefore needed to develop factorial benchmark datasets and physics-informed decision tools that recommend voltage, dose/current, dead time, geometry, exposure/binning, filters, and phase lists while reporting expected uncertainty and indexing fidelity.
