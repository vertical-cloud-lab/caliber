## Cleavage of Bulk Single-Crystal MgO Along {100}: Techniques, Reliability, Failure Modes, and Quantitative Properties

### Background

Magnesium oxide (MgO, periclase) crystallizes in the NaCl-type rock-salt structure (space group Fm3̅m), which provides three mutually perpendicular families of {100} cleavage planes (langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 1-8, schultz1994singlecrystalcleavage pages 1-3). Because each {100} plane is electrostatically neutral—alternating cations and anions across the surface—it satisfies the charge-neutrality exclusionary criterion for cleavage in ionic crystals, whereas {111} planes are non-neutral and cannot cleave (schultz1994singlecrystalcleavage pages 6-9). The {100} cleavage toughness of MgO is 0.81 MPa·m^(1/2), derived from Gilman's (1960) measurements and tabulated by Schultz, Jensen, and Bradt (1994) among other cubic structures (schultz1994singlecrystalcleavage pages 11-13). This is roughly three times larger than the cleavage toughness of softer halide crystals such as KBr (0.12 MPa·m^(1/2)) or NaCl (0.17 MPa·m^(1/2)), but still well below 1 MPa·m^(1/2) and therefore in the regime where cleavage proceeds readily once an adequate crack is initiated (schultz1994singlecrystalcleavage pages 13-15).

The experimental {100} surface energies reported in the literature cluster around 1.0–1.2 J/m². Gilman (1960) measured 1.20 J/m² by monocrystal cleavage, while Westwood and Goldheim (1963) obtained values in the range 1.04–1.15 J/m² (boffi1976onthecleavage pages 5-8). The literature average of six published values on {100} is 1.26 J/m² (jandacka2011fractureenergyof pages 5-7). Boffi and Ricci (1976) calculated a theoretical cleavage surface energy of 890 erg/cm² (0.89 J/m²) for {100}, noting that the predicted easy-cleavage direction and energy magnitude are consistent with experiment (boffi1976onthecleavage pages 5-8). ### (1) Razor Blade / Chisel Technique and Reliability for a 10 × 10 × 10 mm Cube

The technique described by SPI Supplies—placing a single-edge razor blade or chisel on a {100} face, aligned parallel to a cube edge, and tapping it with a small hammer—is consistent with widespread laboratory practice for cleaving MgO. Multiple research groups have adopted a nearly identical procedure. Weber et al. (2023) state that "MgO single crystals were freshly cleaved using a razor blade" just before experiments, with loose debris then removed by blowing nitrogen gas over the surface (weber2023armoringofmgo pages 2-4). Bracco et al. (2024) and Meneses et al. (2025) independently describe the same procedure, cleaving MgO with a razor blade immediately before measurements, sometimes inside a glovebag or glovebox to control atmosphere (meneses2025inhibitionofreaction pages 2-3).

In the primary research literature, Futagami and Akashi (1973) cleaved MgO blocks approximately 7 × 7 × 7 mm³ at room temperature using "a chisel fitted with a sharp knife edge." The knife edge was aligned along [001] on the (010) face, and a blow was applied in the [010] direction, producing (100) cleavage slabs approximately 1 mm thick (futagami1973studyonthe pages 1-5). Langdon and Pask (1968) noted that MgO can be cleaved on a soft pad of tissue paper to produce surfaces free of micro-cracks (langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 12-15).

**Reliability for a 10 × 10 × 10 mm cube:** The razor-blade-and-tap method is generally reliable for producing {100} cleavage in MgO cubes of this size, because (a) MgO's {100} cleavage toughness of 0.81 MPa·m^(1/2) is low enough for a sharp blade impact to initiate unstable fracture (schultz1994singlecrystalcleavage pages 11-13), and (b) once initiated, the crack is rapidly redirected by the crystal structure onto the preferred {100} cleavage plane, even if the blade is not perfectly aligned (schultz1994singlecrystalcleavage pages 9-11). However, the outcome is not guaranteed to be a single, atomically flat plane. Common complications include chipping at the blade contact point, cleavage steps from multiple crack origins, and potential deviation of the crack front from the intended direction (futagami1973studyonthe pages 5-11). For a 10 mm cube cleaved in half, the geometry is favorable because the crack path length is modest relative to the crystal size. Attempting to take a thin slab (< 1 mm) from the edge is considerably more challenging and prone to shattering or multiple fracture.

### (2) Recommended Tool, Impact, Support, and Geometry

**Tool:** A sharp, single-edge razor blade or a chisel with a fine knife edge is the standard tool. The razor blade must be fresh and sharp, as MgO has a Mohs hardness of 5.5 (jandacka2011fractureenergyof pages 2-4) and a Knoop hardness of approximately 11 GPa (everitt1990indentationcreepand pages 74-81), meaning it can rapidly dull a blade. Futagami and Akashi specifically used "a chisel fitted with a sharp knife edge" (futagami1973studyonthe pages 1-5). Preuss et al. (2026) refined diamond-wire-sawn notches using "a sharp razor blade and 1 µm diamond paste" to prepare pre-cracks in MgO DCDC specimens, demonstrating that a razor blade is adequate for creating fine starter notches (preuss2026stablecrackpropagation pages 3-6).

**Impact:** A light but sharp tap is preferable to a heavy blow. MgO is brittle with a low cleavage toughness (0.81 MPa·m^(1/2)), so only modest force is needed to nucleate a crack; excessive force risks shattering the crystal or generating excessive plastic deformation beneath the blade (futagami1973studyonthe pages 16-19, futagami1973studyonthe pages 1-5). Futagami and Akashi applied a single blow that generated a rapidly propagating cleavage crack (futagami1973studyonthe pages 1-5). A small jeweler's hammer or similar light instrument is appropriate.

**Support:** The crystal should be placed on a compliant substrate rather than a rigid anvil. Langdon and Pask (1968) specifically recommend cleaving MgO "on a soft pad of tissue paper" to prevent surface micro-cracking on the bottom face and unwanted fracture from reflected stress waves (langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 12-15). A hard anvil can generate Hertzian contact damage, introduce reflected tensile waves, and degrade the cleavage surface.

**Geometry:** Splitting the crystal approximately in half is the most reliable geometry. For a 10 × 10 × 10 mm cube, the blade should be placed at the midpoint of one face, aligned along a <100> direction. Taking thin slabs near an edge becomes increasingly difficult below about 1 mm thickness, which was the minimum slab thickness reported by Futagami and Akashi for 7 mm cubes (futagami1973studyonthe pages 1-5). MgO's relatively high cleavage toughness compared to other NaCl-structure crystals means that thinner slabs require more precise initiation and are prone to multiple fracture.

### (3) Documented Causes of Failed or Poor Cleaves

Multiple mechanisms have been documented in the primary literature as causes of imperfect cleavage in MgO:

**Plastic deformation and {110}⟨1̅10⟩ dislocation slip under the blade.** MgO's primary room-temperature slip system is {110}⟨1̅10⟩ (langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 1-8, langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 8-12). The concentrated stress field beneath the blade or chisel can activate slip on these planes before the crack propagates, generating dislocations that absorb energy and interfere with clean cleavage. Futagami and Akashi (1973) observed that cleavage activates 45° slip bands on (110) planes, and that fresh edge dislocations with ½⟨110⟩-type Burgers vectors are nucleated near the fracture surface, especially at large cleavage steps (futagami1973studyonthe pages 1-5, futagami1973studyonthe pages 16-19). The plastic work contribution at room temperature is estimated to be only a few percent of the intrinsic surface energy but can become significant when crack velocity changes (futagami1973studyonthe pages 16-19).

**Dislocation nucleation and multiplication at the crack front.** Concentrated stress at the crack tip nucleates fresh dislocations rather than merely mobilizing pre-existing ones. These dislocations form long parallel arrays near major cleavage steps and can slow or arrest crack propagation (futagami1973studyonthe pages 5-11). Preuss et al. (2026) directly observed cracks decelerating to 15% of their initial velocity when entering dislocation-rich regions in MgO (preuss2026stablecrackpropagation pages 1-3).

**Cleavage steps from multiple crack origins.** When cracks initiate at different microscopic sites and propagate simultaneously on slightly displaced parallel planes, one crack front thrusts into the other, producing large cleavage steps. River lines on the fracture surface indicate changing propagation paths (futagami1973studyonthe pages 5-11).

**Kink bands and Stroh cracks.** Nonhomogeneous deformation creates kink bands that act as crack origins. Stroh cracks form when piled-up edge dislocations generate sufficient stress concentration at a barrier, nucleating cracks on conjugate {110} slip planes rather than on the intended {100} cleavage plane (langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 8-12, langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 12-15).

**Crack deflection by slip bands.** Propagating cleavage cracks are slowed when cutting through regions densely populated by screw dislocations; slip bands can impede crack growth in certain directions and redirect the crack path. A secondary crack bounded by slip bands may reorient before entering the cleavage plane (sarfarazi1987onthemicromechanical pages 10-12).

**Sub-grain boundaries.** Bulk MgO crystals typically contain grown-in dislocation densities of approximately 10^6 cm⁻² organized into sub-grains of roughly 1 mm² (futagami1973studyonthe pages 1-5). Although Futagami and Akashi (1973) found no clear relationship between observed fresh-dislocation regions and sub-grain boundaries despite many grown-in dislocations in those boundaries (futagami1973studyonthe pages 5-11), sub-grain boundaries can tilt the local lattice orientation and potentially cause crack deflection at the boundary.

**Surface damage and micro-cracks.** Pre-existing edge micro-cracks or handling damage on the crystal surface provide uncontrolled fracture origins. Even cracks smaller than the critical Griffith size can grow once slip occurs and lead to fracture from unintended locations (langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 12-15). This is why cleaving on a soft pad is important (langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 12-15).

**Dull blade.** MgO's Mohs hardness of 5.5 and Knoop hardness of ~11 GPa mean that a dull blade distributes force over a larger area, promotes plastic deformation over fracture initiation, and increases the likelihood of crushing rather than cleaving. A fresh, sharp edge is essential.

### (4) Effect of Pre-Scoring or Sawing a Notch on Cleavage Initiation

Pre-scoring or sawing a guide notch generally **helps** cleavage initiation in MgO by providing a controlled stress concentrator at the desired location and orientation. Schultz et al. (1994) state that fracture toughness measurements on single crystals are commonly made "by some form of indentation pre-cracking, or by utilizing guide notches to force the incipient crack to initiate on the crystallographic plane of interest." After extending only a short distance, propagating cracks in single crystals are redirected by the crystal structure onto the preferred cleavage plane (schultz1994singlecrystalcleavage pages 9-11).

Langdon and Pask (1968) demonstrated that surface flaws serve as fracture origins in MgO: damaged or cleaved surfaces produced fracture origins at the crystal periphery coincident with surface flaws. Conversely, polishing away such cracks increased ductility five- to tenfold (langdon1968mechanicalbehaviorof pages 12-15). This confirms that a deliberately introduced notch or score lowers the stress needed for cleavage initiation.

In recent work, Preuss et al. (2026) prepared MgO DCDC test specimens by first drilling a central hole, then cutting opposing notches (~0.5 mm deep) using diamond wire threaded through the hole. The sawed notches were subsequently refined using a sharp razor blade and 1 µm diamond paste to create well-defined pre-cracks (preuss2026stablecrackpropagation pages 3-6). This two-step approach—coarse sawing followed by blade-refinement—represents best practice for controlled crack initiation in MgO.

**Caveats:** While a notch assists initiation, sawing introduces a damage zone containing dislocations and micro-cracks in the near-surface region (futagami1973studyonthe pages 5-11). The width and depth of this damage zone depend on the cutting method and speed. For simple laboratory cleavage (as opposed to fracture-mechanics testing), a light score with a sharp blade or diamond scribe along the intended {100} cleavage line, followed by a tap with a sharp blade at that score, is preferable to deep sawing. The score should be shallow enough to avoid introducing heavy subsurface plasticity but deep enough to serve as a reliable stress concentrator. Futagami and Akashi's observation that the crack front can deviate from the nominal loading direction (futagami1973studyonthe pages 5-11) underscores that even with a guide notch, the cleavage path is not guaranteed to be perfectly straight, particularly in crystals with high grown-in dislocation densities or sub-grain structure.

### Summary of Practical Recommendations

For cleaving a 10 × 10 × 10 mm MgO single crystal along {100} at room temperature in air:

1. Use a fresh, sharp single-edge razor blade or fine-edged chisel.
2. Place the crystal on a compliant pad (tissue paper or soft polymer mat).
3. Align the blade edge precisely along a ⟨100⟩ direction on the target {100} face.
4. Optionally pre-score lightly with a diamond scribe along the intended cleavage line to guide initiation.
5. Apply a single, light, sharp tap with a small hammer.
6. Splitting approximately in half is more reliable than attempting thin slabs; slabs thinner than ~1 mm become increasingly difficult.
7. Remove loose debris by blowing with dry nitrogen or compressed air (weber2023armoringofmgo pages 2-4).

The method is broadly reliable for MgO given its low cleavage toughness (0.81 MPa·m^(1/2)) and perfect {100} cleavage, but the user should expect occasional cleavage steps, river lines, and minor surface damage, particularly if the blade is dull, the crystal contains sub-grain boundaries, or the impact force is excessive.
