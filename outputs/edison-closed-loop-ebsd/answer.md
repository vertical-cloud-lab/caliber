Question: Systematic prior-art survey: closed-loop, automated, or algorithm-driven optimization of EBSD (electron backscatter diffraction) data-processing and indexing parameters.

Specifically map what has been published on:
1. Automated or optimizer-driven tuning of EBSD pattern-processing or indexing parameters (background correction, binning, neighbour averaging, Hough transform settings such as band count / rho-theta smoothing, dictionary indexing settings), using Bayesian optimization, Gaussian processes, genetic/evolutionary algorithms, grid/random search, or reinforcement learning.
2. Feedback loops that re-index stored raw EBSD patterns and use quality metrics (confidence index CI, image quality IQ, mean angular deviation / fit, normalized cross-correlation NCC) as an objective function to select the next parameter set.
3. Work that optimizes against GRAIN-LEVEL or microstructure-level outcomes (grain count stability vs misorientation threshold, fraction of speckle/wild grains, grain boundary integrity, fraction of pixels altered by cleanup) rather than only per-pixel pattern-level scores — i.e., multi-objective or constrained optimization of EBSD processing.
4. Hybrid indexing strategies (Hough first, dictionary indexing on low-quality residue) and any published AUTOMATED criteria for when to escalate from Hough to dictionary/pattern-matching indexing.
5. Software tools or frameworks claiming autonomous, self-driving, or human-out-of-the-loop EBSD post-processing or characterization workflows, including anything connecting post-processing results back to SEM acquisition-parameter recommendations (beam current, dwell time, step size).

For each area, identify the closest published work with citations, state clearly what it does and does NOT automate, and conclude with an assessment of which of the five areas remain unclaimed gaps in the literature.

# Systematic prior-art survey: closed-loop optimization of EBSD processing and indexing

## Scope and search interpretation

The survey distinguished four often-confounded categories: **(i)** optimization of pattern-processing or indexing parameters, **(ii)** algorithmic refinement of orientation or pattern-center estimates for a fixed pattern, **(iii)** automated post-indexing map cleanup/segmentation, and **(iv)** automation of acquisition or registration. Only the first category squarely satisfies area 1; automation in the other categories is relevant as neighboring prior art but does not establish a closed-loop EBSD indexing optimizer.

Searches covered EBSD with Bayesian optimization, Gaussian processes, evolutionary/genetic algorithms, particle swarm optimization, grid/random search, reinforcement learning, Hough parameters, dictionary indexing, reindexing, CI/IQ/MAD/fit/NCC, grain-level objectives, autonomous microscopy, and acquisition feedback. Patent searches returned no relevant EBSD patent in the searched corpus. Absence findings below therefore mean **“not found in this systematic search,” not a legal opinion of novelty or freedom to operate**.

## 1. Optimizer-driven tuning of pattern-processing or indexing parameters

### Closest demonstrated work: Polonsky et al. (2019)

Polonsky and colleagues integrated DREAM.3D into the cloud-based BisQue platform so that pipeline variables could be parameterized and executions parallelized. Their principal EBSD example varied the **post-indexing grain-segmentation misorientation tolerance** from 0.1° to 10° in 0.1° increments, producing 100 three-dimensional reconstructions while holding minimum-neighbor and minimum-defect-size settings fixed. They evaluated the fraction of CI-masked “good” voxels retained and total reconstructed grain count, then selected 2.0° from convergence/elbow behavior in the derivatives of those metrics. This is genuine automated parameter sweeping with microstructure-level evaluation. However, it operates on already indexed orientation data; the study states that raw EBSD patterns were not collected for that dataset. It therefore does **not** tune background correction, detector binning, neighbor averaging, Hough band count, Hough rho/theta smoothing, or dictionary-search settings, and it cannot re-index patterns under candidate settings. (polonsky2019threedimensionalanalysisand pages 1-2, polonsky2019threedimensionalanalysisand pages 5-6, polonsky2019threedimensionalanalysisand pages 6-8)

### Adjacent work that should not be overread

Shen et al. developed CNN-based orientation indexing and optimized neural-network architecture/training for speed and prediction accuracy. The trained model indexes patterns directly and uses quaternion-norm deviation as a reliability measure. This replaces or supplements conventional indexing; it does not run Bayesian, evolutionary, grid, or reinforcement-learning search over conventional EBSD processing parameters. It also remains sensitive to experimental geometry and requires detector/setup parameters to be determined beforehand or accommodated through retraining. (shen2019convolutionalneuralnetworkbased pages 1-2, shen2019convolutionalneuralnetworkbased pages 8-10, shen2019convolutionalneuralnetworkbased pages 10-12)

Kaufmann et al. automated phase classification from individual EBSD patterns with a CNN. The work reduces human decisions in phase mapping and outperforms conventional Hough-plus-user-selected phase/chemistry workflows in difficult examples. The “optimization” described for the conventional comparator was repeated **user adjustment** of EDS reference chemistry against a microstructure already known in advance, not an algorithmic search over EBSD settings. The CNN likewise classifies patterns rather than optimizing background, Hough, binning, or dictionary parameters. (kaufmann2020phasemappingin pages 1-2, kaufmann2020phasemappingin pages 6-7)

Global/evolutionary optimization has also been published for extracting or refining orientation, pattern center, and deformation state from pattern matching. That is important neighboring art: it establishes optimizer use **inside a per-pattern inverse problem**. It is not equivalent to an outer loop choosing data-processing/indexing hyperparameters from map-quality or grain-quality outcomes. The retrieved full-text evidence did not support treating such work as prior art for the broader closed-loop parameter-selection architecture.

**Assessment:** No verified publication was found that applies Bayesian optimization, a GP surrogate, genetic/evolutionary search, random/grid search, RL, or a comparable optimizer to the listed **pattern-processing and indexing knobs** and automatically selects the best configuration. Area 1 remains substantially open, aside from adjacent per-pattern refinement and post-indexing segmentation sweeps.

## 2. Re-indexing stored raw patterns in a metric-driven feedback loop

Stinville et al. provide a useful enabling precedent: their archived 3D dataset includes approximately 1.71 TB of raw `.up2` Kikuchi patterns, and the paper explicitly states that these can be opened and reprocessed with EMsoft dictionary indexing, EMSphInx spherical-pattern reindexing, or commercial EBSD software. The indexed files also expose IQ and CI. This demonstrates the technical availability of stored-pattern reindexing, but the paper is a dataset publication, not an optimizer: it does not generate candidate parameter sets, score them, or select the next experiment. (stinville2022multimodaldatasetof pages 9-12, stinville2022multimodaldatasetof pages 12-13)

Shen et al. provide the closest feedback-like decision rule. They propose obtaining a preliminary CNN orientation and confidence at every point, performing only a local dictionary search around high-confidence predictions, and performing a full dictionary search for low-confidence predictions. The confidence proxy is the norm of the predicted quaternion. This is automated computational routing, but it is not a demonstrated iterative loop over processing/indexing parameter sets, and it does not use CI, IQ, MAD/fit, or NCC to propose the next candidate configuration. (shen2019convolutionalneuralnetworkbased pages 10-12)

Polonsky et al. use CI to define an initial mask of good voxels and assess how many remain after cleanup and segmentation. CI is therefore part of a reconstruction-level quality assessment, but it is not fed back to re-index raw patterns; indeed, raw patterns were unavailable in that example. (polonsky2019threedimensionalanalysisand pages 5-6)

**Assessment:** No demonstrated publication was found with the complete loop **candidate parameters → re-index stored raw patterns → compute CI/IQ/MAD/fit/NCC objective → choose the next parameter set → repeat**. Area 2 remains an unclaimed gap in the searched literature.

## 3. Grain- or microstructure-level objectives

This area has the strongest direct prior art, but it is narrow.

Polonsky et al. explicitly recognized that segmentation tolerance changes grain consolidation and retained data in opposite directions. Their two high-level metrics were total grain count and the fraction of initially CI-qualified voxels retained through reconstruction. They chose the lowest tolerance at which the changes in both quantities became approximately constant, analogous to an elbow criterion. This is a microstructure-level, effectively two-metric parameter-selection procedure rather than optimization of a per-pattern score. (polonsky2019threedimensionalanalysisand pages 5-6, polonsky2019threedimensionalanalysisand pages 6-8)

Staněk et al. independently show why such objectives matter. They analyzed the dependence of 2D and 3D grain segmentation on misorientation threshold, including mean grain counts and the formation of erroneously merged giant grains. For one noisy experimental dataset, changing noise removal and excluding grains below eight voxels changed the detected population from more than one million grains to 74,579. This is a systematic sensitivity study and supplies possible grain-level observables, but it does not implement an autonomous optimizer or select cleanup parameters through a formal multi-objective rule. (stanek2020comparisonofsegmentation pages 1-2, stanek2020comparisonofsegmentation pages 7-9)

Neither publication demonstrates optimization across a broad EBSD-processing chain using objectives such as wild-grain/speckle fraction, boundary continuity or integrity, grain-count stability over threshold perturbations, fraction of pixels modified by cleanup, or a constraint on topology preservation. Polonsky varies essentially one segmentation parameter, keeps other settings fixed, and identifies a balance/elbow rather than computing a Pareto front or solving a constrained optimization problem. (polonsky2019threedimensionalanalysisand pages 5-6)

**Assessment:** Area 3 is **partially occupied**. A broad claim to “optimizing EBSD processing using grain-level outcomes” would encounter Polonsky et al. A narrower architecture that jointly tunes indexing and cleanup parameters using several topology- and intervention-aware grain objectives still appears open in the searched literature.

## 4. Hybrid Hough-first/dictionary-on-residue indexing

The closest automated escalation rule is Shen et al., but its first stage is a CNN rather than Hough indexing. Their proposed rule is explicit: high-confidence CNN results receive dictionary matching only near the preliminary orientation; low-confidence results receive full dictionary search. Thus, the publication anticipates confidence-triggered allocation of expensive pattern matching. It does not demonstrate a Hough-first pipeline, does not define residue using CI/IQ/MAD/NCC from Hough indexing, and does not report an executed end-to-end selective reindexing experiment. (shen2019convolutionalneuralnetworkbased pages 10-12)

The same paper compares CNN and Hough indexing at different exposure times. CNN indexing produced a larger fraction of points below a 10° misorientation-error cutoff at high noise, whereas Hough was more accurate among the smaller subset it indexed correctly. This comparison motivates selective hybridization, but the reported hybrid suggestion remains CNN-to-dictionary rather than Hough-to-dictionary. (shen2019convolutionalneuralnetworkbased pages 8-10, shen2019convolutionalneuralnetworkbased pages 10-12)

Kaufmann et al. likewise replace conventional Hough phase assignment with CNN classification; they do not retain Hough as a first pass and selectively escalate failed pixels. (kaufmann2020phasemappingin pages 1-2, kaufmann2020phasemappingin pages 6-7)

**Assessment:** Confidence-triggered hybrid search is neighboring prior art, so a very broad claim to “use confidence to decide whether to run dictionary matching” is not open. However, no verified demonstration was found of **Hough first → automatically identify low-quality residue → dictionary/pattern-match only that residue**, particularly using an explicit CI/IQ/MAD/NCC or grain-context escalation criterion. Area 4 therefore remains mostly open in the requested Hough-specific form.

## 5. Autonomous EBSD workflows and acquisition feedback

Several publications automate substantial sub-workflows:

* **BisQue–DREAM.3D:** cloud execution, provenance, parallel pipeline runs, and range parameterization for 3D reconstruction. Parameters and ranges are supplied rather than autonomously discovered, and the demonstrated optimization begins after indexing. (polonsky2019threedimensionalanalysisand pages 1-2, polonsky2019threedimensionalanalysisand pages 5-6)
* **Automated serial sectioning:** Burnett et al. describe a fully automated PFIB serial-section routine using automatically recognized tracking fiducials and repeated milling/imaging positions. Yet acceptable milling current was determined experimentally, and the authors state that systematic mapping of voltage/current space remained necessary. EBSD settings—including beam energy/current, dwell, binning, band count, and Hough settings—were fixed by the investigators rather than adapted from post-processing results. (burnett2016largevolumeserial pages 4-6, burnett2016largevolumeserial pages 6-7)
* **Automated registration and distortion correction:** Sparks et al. use automated sample alignment, pattern-center fitting for each section, CI thresholding, and optimizer-based multimodal registration. The optimizer maximizes an image-registration similarity metric and transforms maps; it does not alter EBSD indexing settings or SEM acquisition conditions. (sparks20243dreconstructionof pages 9-12, sparks20243dreconstructionof pages 23-24)
* **SE–EBSD map correction:** Winiarski et al. automate aspects of co-registration and long-/short-range distortion correction using trainable filters and moving least squares. The paper explicitly concludes that more automated procedures and more robust accuracy measures still need development. It also discusses faster or smart acquisition as a prospect, not as a closed post-processing-to-acquisition controller. (winiarski2021correctionofartefacts pages 4-6, winiarski2021correctionofartefacts pages 15-17)
* **CNN indexing/phase mapping:** Shen and Kaufmann automate inference from patterns, but training data, instrument calibration, phase standards, and/or model selection remain external; neither closes the loop to microscope settings. (shen2019convolutionalneuralnetworkbased pages 1-2, shen2019convolutionalneuralnetworkbased pages 8-10, kaufmann2020phasemappingin pages 1-2)

No retrieved work used post-processing outcomes to recommend or automatically change **beam current, pattern dwell/exposure, detector binning, or spatial step size** for a subsequent SEM/EBSD acquisition. Burnett et al. come closest to connecting acquisition conditions with indexing rate and band contrast, but the comparison is an investigator-designed experiment, not a controller: selected ion currents are evaluated under fixed EBSD conditions and an acceptable current is chosen experimentally. (burnett2016largevolumeserial pages 4-6, burnett2016largevolumeserial pages 6-7)

**Assessment:** Automated components and scripted pipelines are well established, so “automated EBSD workflow” alone is not a gap. A genuinely autonomous, human-out-of-the-loop EBSD characterization system that interprets indexed/map/grain quality and then recommends or controls SEM acquisition parameters remains open in the searched literature.

## Consolidated gap assessment

1. **Optimizer-driven tuning of EBSD pattern-processing/indexing settings — open.** Neighboring art covers per-pattern inverse optimization, ML indexing, and post-indexing segmentation sweeps, but not outer-loop optimization of background, binning, averaging, Hough, or dictionary hyperparameters.
2. **Metric-driven iterative reindexing of stored raw patterns — open.** Raw-pattern reindexing is enabled and confidence-directed search is proposed, but the complete iterative objective loop was not found.
3. **Grain-level optimization — partially claimed.** Polonsky et al. directly cover segmentation-tolerance selection using retained-good-data fraction and grain count. Broad multi-objective, constrained, topology-aware optimization across indexing plus cleanup remains open.
4. **Automated hybrid escalation — partially anticipated, Hough-specific implementation open.** Shen et al. anticipate confidence-triggered local versus full dictionary search after CNN indexing. A demonstrated Hough-first, dictionary-on-residue workflow with formal escalation criteria was not found.
5. **Autonomous workflow with feedback to acquisition — open at the system level.** Serial sectioning, registration, reconstruction, correction, and ML inference are individually automated, but no end-to-end post-processing-to-SEM acquisition feedback system was found.

The most defensible unexplored combination is therefore: **repeated reindexing of archived raw patterns under optimizer-selected processing/indexing settings; joint pattern-, pixel-, and grain-level objectives with explicit cleanup/topology constraints; confidence- and grain-context-based escalation from Hough to dictionary matching; and translation of the optimized result into acquisition recommendations or microscope control.** None of the verified publications combines those elements into a closed, autonomous EBSD loop.
