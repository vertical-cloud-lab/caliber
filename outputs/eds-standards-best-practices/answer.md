# Best Practices for Creating and Using EDS Standards for Bulk SEM-EDS Quantification

## Primary Recommended Reference

The single most comprehensive published source addressing the creation, use, and reuse of standards for bulk SEM-EDS quantification is **Newbury and Ritchie (2015), "Performing elemental microanalysis with high accuracy and high precision by scanning electron microscopy/silicon drift detector energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (SEM/SDD-EDS)," *Journal of Materials Science* 50:493–518** (DOI: 10.1007/s10853-014-8685-2; NIST public domain/open access). With over 600 citations, this paper provides measured evidence that SDD-EDS achieves accuracy and precision equivalent to WDS when the standards-based k-ratio protocol is followed (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 1-2, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 2-4). It explicitly addresses archived standard reuse, dead-time management, surface preparation, low-kV considerations, and quantitative performance benchmarks across dozens of materials.

## 1. Temporal Validity and Reuse of Archived Standard Spectra

The stability of SDD-EDS technology is the key enabler for archived-standard reuse. Newbury and Ritchie (2015) explicitly state that the stability of SDD-EDS detectors enables analysts to operate with previously measured and stored standard spectra, provided a quality assurance protocol confirms that current measurement conditions match those under which archived standards were measured (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 11-13). SDD-EDS resolution and calibration are described as "nearly constant over the entire input count rate range," with peak shape and position remaining consistent regardless of variations in count rate from major to trace constituents (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 9-11). This stability arises because SDD-EDS operates at approximately −20 °C using Peltier cooling (versus −190 °C with liquid nitrogen for Si(Li)-EDS), achieving equal or better resolution performance with dramatically higher throughput (up to 130,000 counts per second) (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 9-11, newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 2-3).

The earlier Si(Li)-EDS era already established the principle of archived standards: "the stability of the EDS meant that the spectra of standards could be archived and recalled as needed" (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 2-4). With SDD-EDS, this stability is further enhanced. The Goldstein et al. (2018) textbook chapter specifically addresses that archived standards may be used across sessions, recommending that a fresh calibration standard spectrum (e.g., Cu) be compared to archived spectra to verify consistency (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 4-6).

However, the literature provides limited specific quantitative data on multi-year drift magnitudes for SDD-EDS (e.g., eV/year of calibration drift, percentage change in counts-per-nA-second over years). The DTSA-II QC Report functionality is recommended for generating control charts to track detector stability over time (goldstein2018energydispersivexray pages 22-25). In practice, the recommendation is to verify—not to assume—stability at each session.

For the user's scenario (reusable per-voltage standards library at 5, 10, 15, 20 kV), archived standards are supported by the literature, provided the per-session QC protocol described below is followed.

## 2. Per-Session Quality Control When Reusing Archived Standards

The recommended re-verification protocol draws from multiple sources:

**Energy calibration zero/gain check.** Energy calibration should be performed consistently before each day's measurements using vendor-recommended procedures. Modern pulse processors perform continuous zero offset calibration and adjust electronic gain based on characteristic X-ray features (goldstein2018energydispersivexray pages 18-20). The peak position and resolution should remain constant regardless of input count rate or dead-time variations (goldstein2018energydispersivexray pages 18-20).

**Resolution/FWHM check.** Detector resolution must not change appreciably across a tenfold or greater variation in X-ray flux (goldstein2018energydispersivexray pages 22-25). DTSA-II QC Reports can systematically compare current fit parameters against historical values, enabling detection of short- or long-term drift (goldstein2018energydispersivexray pages 22-25).

**Counts-per-nA-second check.** Measured X-ray intensity must scale linearly with effective probe dose—twice the dose should produce twice the measured intensity. The validation procedure involves plotting intensity divided by probe current versus probe current; a horizontal line confirms proper detector performance (goldstein2018energydispersivexray pages 18-20).

**Duane-Hunt limit check.** The spectrum should extend from approximately 100 eV to the Duane-Hunt limit corresponding to the incident beam energy, providing an independent confirmation that the actual accelerating voltage matches the set point (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4). This is particularly important when reusing archived standards at fixed voltage set points.

**Drift-correction practices.** If deviations are detected, recalibration and fresh standard acquisition are necessary. There is no accepted practice for mathematically "correcting" an archived standard for drift—the standard should simply be re-measured.

## 3. Dose Normalization and Probe-Current Measurement

The k-ratio protocol requires knowledge of the electron dose (beam current × live time) for both the unknown and the standard (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4). Beam current measurement via Faraday cup is identified as critical for the standards-based protocol, though it presents implementation challenges on SEM platforms that may require electrical isolation of the specimen stage (newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 28-29). Periodic Faraday cup measurements or picoammeter installations are recommended (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 4-6).

When absolute probe current measurement is impractical, an alternative approach uses a known material's spectrum as a substitute: because measured integrated intensity scales linearly with dose, a QC standard's count rate can serve as a dose-normalization reference (newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 28-29, newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 29-29). This is particularly relevant for SEM platforms (like the user's Apreo) that may lack a built-in Faraday cup. In such cases, measuring a QC material (e.g., Cu or a NIST SRM glass) at the beginning and end of each session provides both a dose-normalization anchor and a detector-stability check.

Beam-current measurement error propagates directly into k-ratios and concentrations: any fractional error in the dose ratio between unknown and standard produces the same fractional error in the k-ratio. This is why dose determination must be treated as a critical measurement, not an afterthought.

The Giunto et al. (2026) work demonstrates a related practical approach: a one-time per-element standard acquisition establishes a permanent reference set for all future analyses on the same instrument, with the analytical total serving as the primary QC metric for reliability (giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 1-3, giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 5-6).

**The dead-time misconception.** A common misconception is that dead time must be identical between standard and unknown acquisitions. The literature is clear that dead time is a parameter to be kept within an acceptable range (≤10% for SDD-EDS quantitative work, more conservative for trace analysis), not one that must match between measurements (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 6-8). What matters is that the dead-time correction is accurate—the live-time clock must properly account for the time the detector is busy—and that the count rate is low enough to avoid significant coincidence artifacts. Because the k-ratio is normalized by dose (beam current × live time), different dead times between standard and unknown are perfectly acceptable as long as each measurement's live time is accurately recorded (goldstein2018energydispersivexray pages 18-20). Some vendor documentation suggesting dead-time matching may cause operators to waste time adjusting beam current unnecessarily between standard and unknown measurements; this is not required by the physics of the measurement.

**Recommended dead-time limits.** For SDD-EDS, approximately ≤10% dead time on the most highly excited standard is recommended to minimize coincidence peaks that can affect trace element detection (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 6-8, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 20-21). This is more conservative than the ≤30% traditionally recommended for older Si(Li)-EDS systems (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 6-8). For trace analysis where coincidence peaks could mimic real trace constituents, even lower dead times are preferred, and some vendors now offer post-acquisition coincidence deconvolution (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 20-21).

## 5. Creating Standards: Materials, Preparation, and Care

**Choice of standard materials.** Pure elements are the most straightforward standards and can be used for most of the periodic table. For elements that are not in suitable solid form at ambient temperature/vacuum, that are highly reactive, that deteriorate under electron bombardment, or that have low melting points, stoichiometric compounds are used instead (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 2-4). Examples include MgO for O and Mg, FeS₂ for S (elemental S is unstable under the beam), KCl for K and Cl, and GaP for Ga and P. For the user's case (AlSi10Mg at 5 kV with Al Kα, Si Kα, Mg Kα): pure Al, Si wafer, and MgO are appropriate standard choices. Importantly, the standard need not closely match the unknown's composition—this is a key advantage of the k-ratio method (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 2-4, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 16-18).

**Homogeneity requirements.** Standards must be verified to be microscopically homogeneous at the micrometer lateral scale before use. Multi-element standards like NIST SRM 470 (K411 glass) and SRM 479 (Fe-Cr-Ni alloy) are certified homogeneous on a microscopic scale and can serve as both standards and QC materials (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 5-6, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 16-18).

**Surface preparation and polishing.** Specimens must be metallographically polished to a high degree of surface finish: surface roughness below 100 nm RMS using final polish with 100 nm abrasives is required; for low-energy photons below 1 keV (relevant to Al Kα at 1.487 keV and Mg Kα at 1.254 keV at 5 kV), surface finish should be better than 50 nm RMS (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 11-13). Chemical polishing and electrochemical etching must be avoided, as they can alter the near-surface composition that is the principal region sampled by electron-excited X-rays (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 2-4).

**Conductive coating matching.** A carbon coating of 8–10 nm thickness applied by thermal evaporation is typically sufficient for insulator surfaces, with negligible effects on accuracy at 10 keV or higher beam energies (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 9-11). However, at low beam energy (E₀ ≤ 5 keV), differences in coating thickness between unknowns and standards can introduce significant quantification errors (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 11-13, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 9-11). For the user's 5 kV case, the conductive coating on standards and unknowns must be matched as closely as possible. Metallic standards (pure Al, Si wafer) that are naturally conductive may not need coating, but if the AlSi10Mg sample requires coating (or if MgO does, being an insulator), all measurements should be performed with consistent coating.

**Special considerations at 5 kV.** At low beam energies, the electron range decreases rapidly (approximately as E₀^1.67) and surface layers comprise a much more significant fraction of the interaction volume (newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 29-29, newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 10-13). Native oxide films on Al and Si (typically 2–5 nm) and hydration layers on MgO become non-negligible relative to the X-ray generation depth at 5 kV. Standards should be freshly polished and coated before archiving spectra at 5 kV, and unknown surfaces should be freshly prepared as well.

**Care and storage.** Standard blocks should be stored in a desiccator to minimize oxidation and hydration. MgO is particularly susceptible to hydration forming Mg(OH)₂. Metallic Al oxidizes rapidly in air, forming a native oxide layer that becomes proportionally more significant at low kV. Repolishing intervals should be determined by the user's experience with surface degradation rates; a practical approach is to repolish and recoat standards whenever the QC check shows unacceptable deviation from archived values.

## Summary for the User's Specific Case

For standards-based k-ratio quantification of laser powder bed fusion AlSi10Mg at 5 kV using Al Kα, Si Kα, and Mg Kα with NIST DTSA-II:

1. **The per-voltage standards library approach is well supported.** Archived SDD-EDS standard spectra can be reused across sessions, provided a QC protocol (energy calibration check, resolution check, dose-normalized count-rate check, Duane-Hunt verification) is run at each session start (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 11-13, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 4-6).

2. **At 5 kV, extra care is needed.** Surface preparation must achieve <50 nm roughness. Carbon coating thickness must be matched between standards and unknowns. Native oxides on Al and hydration of MgO are proportionally more significant at low kV (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 11-13, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 9-11, newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 29-29).

3. **Dead time need not match between standard and unknown**—it should simply stay below ~10% for quantitative work (goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 6-8).

4. **Dose must be tracked.** If a Faraday cup is unavailable, normalize dose via a QC standard count rate measured at the start of each session (newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 28-29).

5. **Pure Al, Si wafer, and MgO are appropriate standards.** Store in a desiccator. Repolish and re-acquire standard spectra when QC checks show drift (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 2-4, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 16-18).

6. **The analytical total is your most important QC metric.** Expect 0.98–1.02 for well-prepared bulk specimens; deviations signal problems with surface preparation, missing elements, or measurement conditions (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 4-5, newbury2004quantitativeelectronprobe pages 17-21).
