| Parameter / factor | Effect on quantitative EDS | Mitigation strategy | Key reference |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gas species: helium** | Lowest-\(Z\) gas considered; produces substantially less elastic scattering and a smaller skirt than water vapor, air, or argon. Helium preserved distinguishable EDS interface profiles under conditions where water vapor did not, although helium behavior is not always captured accurately by simple skirt-radius models. | Prefer He when the instrument permits it; still use the lowest pressure and shortest gas path that suppress charging. Validate with a homogeneous reference material because low scattering does not mean zero scattering. | Khouchaf et al. reported accurate results under suitable He conditions; Arnoult et al. demonstrated superior interface-profile resolution with He (pqac-00000018, pqac-00000022) |
| **Gas species: water vapor** | More scattering than He; pressure-dependent loss of focused-probe current and increased excitation outside the intended point. Water vapor is advantageous for charge neutralization and hydrated specimens but can broaden EDS profiles by hundreds of micrometers. | Minimize vapor pressure and gas-path length; use an analytical cone or pressure-limiting aperture close to the specimen. Avoid assuming that charge-neutralizing pressure is analytically harmless. | Water-vapor broadening and the benefit of a restricted 2 mm gas path were demonstrated by Arnoult et al.; pressure effects were modeled by Mansour et al. (pqac-00000018, pqac-00000021) |
| **Gas species: air / nitrogen** | Intermediate scattering. In the pressure-variation approximation, the surviving unscattered fraction decreases approximately as \(f_p=\exp[-0.035\tilde ZPL/(TE_0)]\); thus higher effective gas atomic number, pressure, and path length reduce focused-beam intensity. | Use the lowest stable pressure; reproduce gas composition, pressure, path length, and beam energy for standards and unknowns. Do not treat nominal probe current as equivalent to focused-probe current. | Le Berre et al. developed and tested pressure-variation corrections in air (pqac-00000024, pqac-00000025) |
| **Gas species: argon** | Largest skirt among He, water vapor, air, and Ar in the cited comparison. At 30 keV, the critical pressure for approximately linear intensity behavior was only 52 Pa for Ar, versus 88 Pa for air, 137 Pa for water vapor, and 974 Pa for He under the model conditions. | Avoid Ar for spatially localized quantitative EDS when a lighter gas is available; if unavoidable, use very low pressure and short path length. | Le Berre et al. quantified the gas-species dependence of critical pressure (pqac-00000024, pqac-00000025) |
| **Chamber pressure** | The mean number of collisions is \(m=\sigma_TnL\), the unscattered fraction is \(e^{-m}\), and the skirt fraction is \(1-e^{-m}\). Increasing pressure raises \(n\), transfers current from the focused core to the skirt, introduces distant-material peaks, and can make X-ray intensity vary nonlinearly with pressure. | Determine the minimum pressure that gives stable charge compensation; establish pressure reproducibility; acquire pressure series if applying a correction rather than relying on a single spectrum. | Danilatos’s scattering treatment and Le Berre et al.’s experimental correction study (pqac-00000009, pqac-00000024, pqac-00000025) |
| **Gas-path length, \(L\)** | Collision probability scales with \(\sigma_TnL\), while lateral displacement also grows with distance. At 20 kV and 66 Pa, shortening the path to about 2 mm reduced the skirt to below 10%; long paths allowed X-rays from particles millimeters away. Gas-path length is the PLA-to-specimen distance and need not equal nominal working distance. | Place the specimen as close as safely possible to the final pressure-limiting aperture; use a long ESD/analytical cone or other geometry giving a short gas path while retaining the required EDS takeoff geometry. | Carlton’s operating-condition study and the PLA/gas-path description (pqac-00000014, pqac-00000017, pqac-00000038) |
| **Beam energy: 20–30 keV** | Scattering is less severe than at low energy. At 20 kV and 66 Pa, approximately 25% of the beam was reported in the skirt. At 30 kV, 400 Pa, and 15 mm working distance, 45% scattered beyond 25 µm and 1.5% beyond 1.5 mm; at 665 Pa these fractions rose to 66% and 8%. Even high-kV operation can therefore excite material millimeters away. | Higher voltage mitigates gas scattering, but may sacrifice the desired specimen interaction-volume resolution and may change which X-ray families are excited. Combine higher voltage with low pressure and minimum gas path. | Carlton’s published scattering examples (pqac-00000014, pqac-00000017) |
| **Beam energy: 10 keV** | At 66 Pa, nearly 50% of the beam was reported in the skirt, compared with about 25% at 20 kV. The focused-probe dose and analytical locality are therefore already seriously compromised at modest low-vacuum pressure. | Lower pressure and path length aggressively; do not infer focused current solely from a Faraday-cup measurement of total transported current. | Carlton’s measured operating-condition dependence (pqac-00000014) |
| **Beam energy: 5 keV** | Elastic and total gas-scattering cross sections increase markedly as energy falls; Danilatos explicitly calculated much stronger low-energy scattering behavior. Since approximately half the beam was already skirted at 10 kV, 66 Pa in Carlton’s geometry, 5 kV is expected to be worse—not a validated numerical extrapolation. No located study demonstrates routine few-percent standards-based VP-EDS accuracy at 5 kV. | For few-percent k-ratio work, prefer high vacuum and a conductive coating. If VP operation is unavoidable, use He, the minimum neutralizing pressure, shortest possible path, identical standards/unknown conditions, pressure-series correction, and independent validation against coated high-vacuum measurements. | Energy-dependent cross sections and lack of a 5-kV VP validation (pqac-00000029, pqac-00000030); by contrast, 5-kV high-vacuum carbon-coated work achieved over 98% of results within ±5% RDEV (pqac-00000053, pqac-00000055) |
| **Skirt radius: hundreds of micrometers** | The focused probe can remain nanometers wide while scattered electrons form a broad analytical skirt. Danilatos calculated a 477 µm half-current radius at 15 keV, 2000 Pa, and 2.1 mm gas path. Such a distribution destroys nominal point-analysis locality on heterogeneous specimens. | Ensure that standards and targets are compositionally homogeneous over an area larger than the analytically significant skirt; minimize pressure and path length rather than relying on the focused-probe diameter. | Danilatos’s measured/modelled beam-profile example (pqac-00000013) |
| **Skirt radius: millimeter tail** | A small current fraction at millimeter radius may be negligible for imaging but significant for minor/trace EDS because strong characteristic peaks from remote phases can masquerade as local trace constituents. Published measurements found 1.5–8% of electrons beyond 1.5 mm, depending on pressure. | Survey a millimeter-scale area around the analysis point; remove or shield dissimilar nearby materials; use standards larger than the skirt footprint; regard unexpected trace peaks as suspect. | Carlton’s particle-analysis measurements and recommendations (pqac-00000014, pqac-00000017) |
| **Faraday-cup / dose bookkeeping** | A Faraday cup may measure total transported current, including electrons that later occupy the skirt. The local focused-probe dose is approximately the total dose multiplied by the pressure-dependent unscattered fraction, while skirt electrons generate additional X-rays elsewhere. Matching only total current does not guarantee equal local excitation. | Measure standards and unknowns with identical gas, pressure, energy, PLA geometry, working distance, detector geometry, and current protocol; characterize focused-beam transmission as a function of pressure. | The k-ratio protocol requires identical known dose and geometry, while VP scattering makes the focused fraction pressure dependent (pqac-00000024, pqac-00000026) |
| **Doehne pressure-variation correction** | Two spectra at different pressures are combined using an empirical background factor \(D\) to extrapolate toward the zero-pressure signal. Results depended strongly on pressure choice and \(D\); deviations reached about 7% under tested 30-kV conditions and became larger for some line ratios. | Use low-pressure pairs where intensity is approximately linear; verify disappearance of known skirt-derived peaks and compare with a high-vacuum control. Treat the method as specimen- and condition-specific. | Le Berre et al. found roughly 0–7% deviations for many 30-kV tests, with larger outliers for unfavorable pressure pairs (pqac-00000025, pqac-00000027, pqac-00000028) |
| **Gauvin two-point correction** | Estimates unscattered-beam intensity from spectra at two pressures and calculated unscattered fractions. Accuracy varied with the selected pair; unfavorable high-pressure pairs produced deviations up to approximately 14%. | Select one low and one sufficiently separated higher pressure; validate against a pressure series rather than assuming any two points are adequate. | Le Berre et al.’s two-point tests (pqac-00000027, pqac-00000028) |
| **Gauvin extrapolation correction** | Fits X-ray intensity versus calculated unscattered fraction using at least five pressures and extrapolates to \(f_p=1\). It was the best-performing tested method: typically about 1–3% from the 0-Pa result at 30 kV, although one cited line-ratio result differed by 5.6%. This validates selected 15–30-kV cases, not 5-kV MgO analysis. | Use a multi-pressure series, stable pressure metrology, and a validated scattering model for the actual gas; retain high-vacuum controls. Do not transfer the demonstrated accuracy automatically to 5 kV. | Le Berre et al. reported approximately 1.1–2.6% typical deviations and identified extrapolation as the preferred method (pqac-00000025, pqac-00000027, pqac-00000028) |
