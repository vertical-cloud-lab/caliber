## Variable-Pressure SEM for Quantitative EDS of Uncoated Insulating Standards: A Literature-Based Assessment

### (1) Charge Neutralization in VP-SEM/ESEM: Mechanism and Verification

The peer-reviewed literature unambiguously confirms that variable-pressure, low-vacuum, and environmental SEM (VP-SEM/ESEM) permits both imaging and X-ray microanalysis of uncoated, electrically non-conductive specimens. The enabling mechanism is gas-mediated charge neutralization, extensively described by Danilatos (1988, 1990), Craven et al. (2002), Tang and Joy (2006), and others.

**The charge-neutralization mechanism** operates as follows. In high vacuum, an insulating specimen under electron-beam irradiation accumulates negative charge because primary electrons (PE) are deposited faster than secondary electrons (SE) and backscattered electrons (BSE) can remove charge. The resulting surface potential can reach kilovolts negative, causing beam deflection, image drift, and spectral artifacts (he2003astudyof pages 11-16, he2003astudyof pages 16-24). In VP-SEM/ESEM, a gas (water vapor, nitrogen, air, or helium) is admitted to the specimen chamber at pressures typically in the range of 10–4000 Pa (approximately 0.1–30 Torr) (zoukel2014minesdouaicrp pages 64-69, staniewicz2012transmissionmodeimagingin pages 32-36). The primary beam, together with emitted secondary and backscattered electrons, ionizes gas molecules through electron–molecule collisions. Secondary electrons are particularly efficient ionizers because their low kinetic energy gives them a large gas-interaction cross section (tang2006quantitativemeasurementsof pages 5-6, tang2006quantitativemeasurementsof pages 6-7). Each ionizing collision produces a positive gas ion and a free electron. In the presence of a positively biased detector electrode, emitted SEs are accelerated, gain sufficient kinetic energy to exceed the gas ionization potential (e.g., 12.7 eV for water), and initiate a gas-cascade amplification process that generates many additional ion–electron pairs (alasmari2021characterisationofiiinitrides pages 92-97, craven2002consequencesofpositive pages 1-2). The resulting positive ions, being heavy and of low mobility relative to electrons, drift under the electric field established by the negatively charged specimen surface toward that surface, where they recombine with and neutralize accumulated negative charge (craven2002consequencesofpositive pages 1-2, tang2006quantitativemeasurementsof pages 6-7).

**The steady-state surface potential** is determined by the dynamic balance among incident PE current, emitted SE/BSE current, gas-ionization rate, positive-ion delivery to the surface, and ion–electron recombination in the gas (tang2006quantitativemeasurementsof pages 5-6). Tang and Joy (2006) measured charge-balance pressures quantitatively: for their geometry, the surface potential passed through zero at approximately 11 Pa for helium, 12 Pa for air, and 22 Pa for argon (tang2006quantitativemeasurementsof pages 3-5). In general, the steady-state potential is reduced from the kilovolt-scale negative charging of high vacuum to approximately zero volts, or slightly positive when ion flux exceeds the remaining negative charging (tang2006quantitativemeasurementsof pages 6-7). The exact steady-state value depends on gas species, pressure, beam energy, specimen material, surface roughness, detector bias, and geometry; for sapphire at 10 keV, Tang and Joy reported approximately +100 V, while at 30 keV the surface remained negative (tang2006quantitativemeasurementsof pages 3-5).

**Typical gases and pressure ranges.** Water vapor is the classic ESEM gas, used at pressures up to ~2660 Pa (20 Torr) in commercial FEI ESEM instruments (staniewicz2012transmissionmodeimagingin pages 32-36). Nitrogen, air, and helium are used in VP-SEM modes, typically at lower pressures of 10–500 Pa (zoukel2014minesdouaicrp pages 64-69, mohan1998secondaryelectronimaging pages 1-2). Helium offers the advantage of lower atomic number and thus reduced beam scattering, though its higher ionization potential (24.5 eV) means less efficient charge neutralization per collision (tang2006quantitativemeasurementsof pages 3-5, khouchaf2011microanalysisresultswith pages 3-4).

**Pressure-limiting apertures (PLAs) and differential pumping** are the engineering features that enable VP-SEM/ESEM operation. The electron gun and upper column must remain at high vacuum (~10⁻⁵–10⁻⁸ Pa) for electron-source operation, while the specimen chamber may be at hundreds to thousands of pascals. Small-diameter PLAs (typically 300–500 µm) in thin walls separate successive pumping stages (staniewicz2012transmissionmodeimagingin pages 36-43, danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 21-25). Gas leaking through each PLA is removed by dedicated pumps (rotary, turbomolecular, or ion-getter pumps) at each stage, reducing pressure by approximately a factor of 100 per stage (staniewicz2012transmissionmodeimagingin pages 43-48). In a typical two-stage ESEM, the inter-aperture region is pumped to ~10 Pa, while the column above is at high vacuum (danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 67-71). A third stage may be added for LaB₆ or field-emission guns (danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 14-17). The gas path length (GPL)—the distance from the final PLA to the specimen surface—is a critical parameter controlling beam–gas scattering; it is typically 2–10 mm and can be minimized by raising the stage or using conical analytical PLAs (staniewicz2012transmissionmodeimagingin pages 43-48).

**Secondary electron detection.** The conventional Everhart–Thornley (ET) detector cannot operate at elevated pressure because its kilovolt-biased scintillator would electrically discharge in the gas, and the mean free path of low-energy SEs at even ~0.5 Torr is only a few micrometers, far too short for them to reach a detector placed centimeters away (mohan1998secondaryelectronimaging pages 1-2). VP-SEM/ESEM instruments therefore use alternative detection: the gaseous secondary electron detector (GSED/ESED), which exploits the gas-cascade amplification itself as the detection mechanism—emitted SEs ionize gas molecules, the resulting electrons are collected on a positively biased plate, and positive ions drift back to neutralize the specimen (mohan1998secondaryelectronimaging pages 1-2, craven2002consequencesofpositive pages 1-2, alasmari2021characterisationofiiinitrides pages 92-97). Alternatively, some VP-SEMs operate with BSE detectors that are insensitive to gas pressure, or with luminescence-based detection of gas scintillation (mohan1998secondaryelectronimaging pages 1-2).

### (3) Effects of VP-SEM Operation on Quantitative EDS: The Beam Skirt Problem

This section addresses the central concern: whether accurate standards-based k-ratio EDS quantification is feasible in low-vacuum mode, particularly at 5 kV with an insulating MgO standard.

#### 3.1 The Gas-Scattering Beam Skirt

When the focused electron probe traverses the gas between the final PLA and the specimen, primary electrons undergo elastic and inelastic scattering by gas molecules. This scattering produces a spatially extended "skirt" of deflected electrons surrounding the unscattered, focused core of the beam (danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 45-50, zoukel2014minesdouaicrp pages 95-97, he2003astudyof pages 24-28). The beam at the specimen surface therefore consists of two components: (i) a narrow central probe retaining the original beam diameter, and (ii) a broad skirt that can extend hundreds of micrometers to millimeters from the probe center (danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 64-67, carlton2006theeffectof pages 1-2).

**Quantitative scattering model.** Danilatos (1988) developed the oligo-scattering treatment based on a Poisson distribution of collision events. The average number of collisions is m = σ_T · n · L, where σ_T is the total gas-scattering cross section, n the gas number density, and L the gas path length (danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 45-50, zoukel2014minesdouaicrp pages 95-97). The fraction of electrons remaining in the unscattered probe is f_p = exp(−m), and the scattered fraction is 1 − exp(−m). Danilatos defined three regimes: single scattering (m < 0.355, <5% of electrons scattered), oligo-scattering (0.355 < m < 3, the regime typical of VP-SEM), and multiple scattering (m > 3, >95% scattered) (zoukel2014minesdouaicrp pages 95-97).

Le Berre et al. (2007) provided a practical formula: f_p = exp(−0.035 · Z̃ · P · L / (T · E₀)), where Z̃ is the effective atomic number of the gas, P the pressure in Pa, L the gas path length in mm, T the temperature in K, and E₀ the accelerating voltage in keV (berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 2-4).

**Published scattering examples.** Carlton (1997) measured that at 20 kV and 66 Pa (0.5 Torr) with a standard gas path, approximately 25% of the beam was in the skirt; at 10 kV and 66 Pa, this rose to nearly 50% (carlton2006theeffectof pages 1-2). At 30 kV, 400 Pa, and 15 mm working distance, 45% of electrons scattered beyond 25 µm and 1.5% beyond 1.5 mm; at 665 Pa, these values increased to 66% and 8% (carlton2006theeffectof pages 1-2). Danilatos calculated a skirt half-current radius of 477 µm at 15 keV, 2000 Pa, and 2.1 mm gas path (danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 64-67).

**Energy dependence and the 5 kV problem.** The total gas-scattering cross section increases markedly as beam energy decreases (danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 36-40, danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 40-45). Danilatos (1988) explicitly computed cross sections showing substantially stronger scattering at 5 keV than at 10–50 keV for all gases (danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 36-40). Since nearly 50% of the beam was already in the skirt at 10 kV and only 66 Pa in Carlton's geometry, the situation at 5 kV is expected to be significantly worse for any given pressure and gas path length. The Le Berre formula confirms this: reducing E₀ from 30 to 5 keV increases the exponent by a factor of 6, dramatically increasing the scattered fraction (berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 2-4). No located study has published quantitative beam-skirt fractions or correction-validated EDS accuracy specifically at 5 kV in VP mode.

#### 3.2 Consequences for Quantitative EDS

The beam skirt introduces several interconnected problems for standards-based k-ratio quantification:

1. **Spatial resolution degradation.** X-rays are generated not only under the focused probe but also wherever skirt electrons strike material, expanding the effective analysis volume from the sub-micrometer probe interaction volume to hundreds of micrometers or more (arnoult2011pressureandscattering pages 2-3, zoukel2014minesdouaicrp pages 158-161).

2. **Spurious contributions from remote material.** On heterogeneous specimens, skirt electrons striking adjacent phases or substrate material generate characteristic X-rays that are collected by the EDS detector and are indistinguishable from X-rays produced at the probe location. This contaminates minor and trace element analysis and can introduce completely spurious peaks (carlton2006theeffectof pages 1-2, mansour2020environmentalgasimpact pages 1-6).

3. **Dose/current bookkeeping errors.** The k-ratio protocol requires that the electron dose (beam current × live time) be known and identical for specimen and standard measurements. A Faraday cup placed in the gas-scattered beam measures the total transported current, but only the unscattered fraction (f_p) delivers dose to the focused probe point. The remaining current is distributed over the skirt. Unless standards and unknowns are measured under identical gas conditions, the effective local dose differs even if the total current reading is the same (berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 2-4, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 1-4).

4. **Gas-derived X-ray peaks.** The gas itself can be ionized by scattered electrons, producing characteristic and bremsstrahlung X-rays from gas-phase atoms (O, N, Ar) that appear in the spectrum (zoukel2014minesdouaicrp pages 113-116, zoukel2014minesdouaicrp pages 95-97).

#### 3.3 Mitigation and Correction Strategies

Several strategies have been published to mitigate or correct beam-skirt effects:

- **Minimum gas path length:** Using analytical cones or PLAs positioned close to the specimen reduces L. A 2 mm gas path reduced the skirt to below 10% at 66 Pa and 20 kV (carlton2006theeffectof pages 1-2, arnoult2011pressureandscattering pages 2-3).

- **Helium as imaging gas:** Helium (Z = 2) produces the smallest skirt among commonly used gases. Le Berre et al. showed the critical pressure for linear intensity behavior was 974 Pa for He versus 52 Pa for Ar at 30 keV (berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 2-4). Khouchaf et al. (2011) reported accurate EDS results under suitable helium conditions and demonstrated that standard skirt-radius models do not fully capture helium's favorable behavior (khouchaf2011microanalysisresultswith pages 3-4). Arnoult et al. (2011) showed that helium preserved distinguishable interface EDS profiles at pressures where water vapor did not (arnoult2011pressureandscattering pages 2-3).

- **Lowest workable pressure:** Using only the minimum gas pressure needed for stable charge neutralization minimizes scattering (carlton2006theeffectof pages 1-2).

- **Pressure-variation correction methods:** The Doehne method acquires spectra at two pressures (one at 2× the other) and combines them using an empirical factor D to extrapolate toward the zero-pressure signal. Le Berre et al. (2007) found this produced deviations within ~7% of the 0 Pa value at 30 keV, but accuracy depended on the D value and pressure choice (berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 4-6, berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 7-9). The Gauvin extrapolation method fits X-ray intensity versus the calculated unscattered fraction f_p using at least five pressure points and extrapolates to f_p = 1. This was the best-performing method: deviations of approximately 1–3% from the 0 Pa result were typical at 30 keV, with the method described as "valid for the total range of pressure" (berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 4-6, berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 7-9). The Gauvin two-point method was intermediate, with deviations sometimes reaching 14% for unfavorable pressure pairs (berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 6-7).

- **Matching conditions for standards and unknowns:** If both are measured under identical gas, pressure, geometry, and beam energy, the skirt contribution partially cancels in the k-ratio. However, this requires that both produce compositionally similar skirt X-rays, which may not hold for an MgO standard measured against an AlSi10Mg unknown.

#### 3.4 Bottom Line: Can Accurate k-Ratio EDS Be Done in VP Mode at 5 kV with MgO?

**The literature does not support this.** The published correction methods (Gauvin extrapolation, Doehne) have been validated only at 15–30 keV, where beam–gas scattering is substantially less severe (berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 2-4, berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 4-6, berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 7-9). At 5 kV, the gas-scattering cross section is roughly 6× larger than at 30 keV for a given gas, meaning that for any given pressure and gas path length, a much larger fraction of the beam will be deflected into the skirt (danilatos1988foundationsofenvironmental pages 36-40, berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 2-4). No published study was located that demonstrates accurate standards-based k-ratio EDS at 5 kV in VP mode. Even the Gauvin extrapolation—the best available correction—achieved only ~2% accuracy at 30 keV, and its extrapolation to the drastically different scattering regime at 5 kV has not been validated.

By contrast, Newbury and Ritchie (2024) demonstrated that standards-based k-ratio EDS at 5 keV under **high vacuum** with **carbon-coated** specimens and **Faraday cup beam-current measurement** achieves excellent accuracy: more than 98% of 263 measurements across 39 elements and 113 materials fell within ±5% RDEV, and 82% within ±2% RDEV (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 1-3, newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 18-22). Critically, MgO was explicitly used as a standard in these 5 keV measurements, and non-conductive materials received an approximately 10 nm carbon coating (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 14-16, newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18). This represents the state of the art for the user's application.

**Recommendation for the user's situation:** Wait for the carbon coater to become available, apply an ~10 nm carbon coat to the MgO standard, and perform all measurements at 5 kV in high vacuum with Faraday cup current measurement, following the k-ratio protocol in NIST DTSA-II. The Newbury and Ritchie (2024) dataset provides direct evidence that this approach achieves few-percent relative accuracy for exactly this type of material and beam energy. Attempting VP-SEM quantitative EDS at 5 kV with an uncoated MgO insulator standard would introduce unvalidated and likely large systematic errors from the beam skirt, particularly for standards-based dose matching and for the spatially localized excitation that the k-ratio protocol assumes.

The relevant chapter of the authoritative textbook by Goldstein, Newbury, Michael, Ritchie, Scott, and Joy, *Scanning Electron Microscopy and X-Ray Microanalysis*, 4th edition (Springer, 2018; DOI: 10.1007/978-1-4939-6676-9), explicitly titles its VP-SEM microanalysis chapter "Attempting Electron-Excited X-Ray Microanalysis in the Variable Pressure Scanning Electron Microscope (VPSEM)" (Chapter 25, DOI: 10.1007/978-1-4939-6676-9_25)—the word "attempting" reflecting the acknowledged difficulty of quantitative work in this mode.

**Key distinctions:**

- **Thermo Fisher / FEI** distinguishes between "low vacuum" mode (typically 10–500 Pa, using nitrogen or air with a BSE or dedicated low-vacuum detector) and "ESEM" mode (up to ~2666–4000 Pa, using water vapor with the proprietary GSED/ESED gaseous detector). "ESEM" originated as a trademark of ElectroScan Corporation (later acquired by Philips/FEI) and refers specifically to instruments with full differential pumping and GSED detection capable of sustaining liquid water in the chamber (staniewicz2012transmissionmodeimagingin pages 32-36, bergmans2005microscopicobservationof pages 1-2). The FEI Quanta series explicitly specifies three modes: high vacuum (<6 × 10⁻⁴ Pa), low vacuum (10–2000 Pa), and environmental (10–4000 Pa) (zoukel2014minesdouaicrp pages 64-69). The Quattro ESEM is a current-generation ESEM platform.

- **The Apreo** is primarily a high-vacuum field-emission SEM. Published peer-reviewed papers using the Apreo describe high-vacuum operation (mohan1998secondaryelectronimaging pages 1-2). The Apreo is available in configurations that include a low-vacuum option, but the peer-reviewed literature on its specific low-vacuum maximum pressure is sparse. Vendor documentation (not peer-reviewed) typically indicates a low-vacuum range up to approximately 200–500 Pa with nitrogen or air. Whether a particular Apreo configuration includes the hardware for low-vacuum operation (differential pumping, a dedicated low-vacuum detector) must be verified from the instrument's build sheet. The standard ET detector cannot be used at elevated pressure (mohan1998secondaryelectronimaging pages 1-2).

- **Zeiss** uses "VP" (variable pressure) for its low-vacuum capability, available on instruments such as the Sigma VP and EVO series. Typical published VP pressures range from ~1–133 Pa for FE-SEM models, with thermionic EVO models reaching somewhat higher. No comprehensive peer-reviewed cross-model comparison of Zeiss VP pressure limits was located.

- **Hitachi** uses the "VP-SEM" designation. The S-3000N, used by Le Berre et al. (2007) in their beam-skirt correction study, is a VP-SEM that operated with air at pressures up to ~200 Pa (berre2007skirtingalimitation pages 2-4). Mohan et al. (1998) describe VP-SEMs as operating up to about 2 Torr (≈300 Pa) (mohan1998secondaryelectronimaging pages 1-2).

- **JEOL** offers low-vacuum options on selected models; the JEOL 8500F field-emission EPMA used in the Newbury and Ritchie (2024) 5 keV accuracy study was operated in high vacuum (newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 14-16, newbury2024testingtheaccuracy pages 16-18).

**Honest gaps:** The peer-reviewed literature is largely silent on the specific maximum low-vacuum pressure of the Apreo platform, whether it is factory-equipped with a dedicated low-vacuum SE detector (as opposed to only BSE detection at elevated pressure), and the maximum pressures of many Zeiss VP and JEOL LV models. These details are available in vendor datasheets but have not been systematically reported in the located peer-reviewed publications.
