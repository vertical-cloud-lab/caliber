| Protocol element | Recommendation | Rationale / literature support |
|---|---|---|
| Specimen preparation | **Primary method:** Embed each independently split subsample in low-viscosity epoxy, vacuum-impregnate, grind, and polish through particle equators to a flat finish. Carbon-coat mounts and standards similarly. Randomize fields and exclude particles intersecting mount edges or pores. Use loose particles only for screening or when polishing is impossible. | Standards-based EDS assumes flat bulk geometry. Polished standards produced approximately 0.08–0.26% relative single-measurement precision and about 0.2% spatial RSD for Si, whereas scratches, shards, and particles generated broad errors approaching an order of magnitude. Normalization to 100% can hide these errors (pqac-00000005, pqac-00000014, pqac-00000017). |
| Accelerating voltage | For polished Al–Si sections, begin at **10 kV** and verify the result at **15 kV** on a subset. Use 15–20 kV only when particles are comfortably larger than the interaction volume and additional count rate is needed. For loose particles, prefer **10 kV** and particles larger than approximately 3–5 µm; 5 kV may reduce geometry effects further but is more sensitive to contamination and low overvoltage. | At 10 kV, approximately 0.9–18 µm glass spheres generally gave particle-to-bulk ratios within 0.9–1.1 above about 1.4 µm. At 15 kV, a 1.8 µm sphere lost about 13% intensity; at 25 kV, 1–2 µm spheres lost 50–75%, and Si Kα particle-to-bulk ratios reached about 1.4. Ten kilovolts gave the lowest variation for irregular shards; 5 kV suffered contamination-related bias (pqac-00000018, pqac-00000072, pqac-00000075, pqac-00000077). |
| Spectrum acquisition | Acquire **at least 10,000 background-corrected Si Kα counts per accepted particle mean**; 20,000–50,000 is preferable when time permits. Keep detector dead time near **10% and below 15%**, monitor beam current with a Faraday cup or reference standard, bracket each session with a homogeneous Al–Si control, and use identical conditions for standards and unknowns. Determine live time empirically from the Si count rate. | Ten thousand net counts limit ideal Poisson relative uncertainty to about 1%, or approximately 0.05–0.06 wt% at 5–6 wt% Si. High-count SDD spectra readily achieve sub-percent repeatability, so geometry, microstructure, and sampling—not counting statistics—should dominate. Excess dead time increases coincidence artifacts; stable beam dose is required for rigorous k-ratios (pqac-00000002, pqac-00000009, pqac-00000010, pqac-00000057). |
| Quantification | Use the existing **standards-based k-ratio/DTSA-II** procedure with pure Al and Si standards and matched beam conditions; retain both normalized composition and the **raw analytical total**. If available, analyze an Al–Si reference alloy near 5–6 wt% Si as independent QC. For loose particles, use a validated **peak-to-local-background method** with particle filtering rather than ordinary bulk ZAF alone. | DTSA-II standards-based analysis placed 84% of 50 major/minor determinations within ±2% relative of reference values. Raw totals near 98–102% are a useful geometry diagnostic; normalization alone can conceal anomalous absorption. Modified P/B methods reduce finite-size and topographic effects but generally retain about 5–10% relative error on raw particles, versus roughly 2–5% for well-prepared standards or polished specimens (pqac-00000008, pqac-00000011, pqac-00000021, pqac-00000044). |
| Per-particle raster or area | On polished sections, raster nearly the **entire exposed particle cross-section**, remaining at least one interaction radius from epoxy and neighboring particles. If whole-section rastering is impractical, combine systematically distributed fields whose total span covers at least **5–10 dendrite or cell spacings**—approximately **10–20 µm** for an AlSi10Mg-like spacing of 2.19 µm—and include the particle center and rim. Do not use one arbitrary point as the particle mean. | Rapidly solidified Al–Si particles contain α-Al cells or dendrites, Si-rich interdendritic eutectic, supersaturated Al, and nanoscale Si. Morphology and spacing can change across one droplet. Reported AlSi10Mg secondary dendrite-arm spacing was 2.19 ± 0.56 µm, while Si was concentrated in interdendritic networks (pqac-00000033, pqac-00000059, pqac-00000060, pqac-00000065). |
| Number of particles per lot | Analyze an initial **30 randomly selected particles per lot**, then estimate between-particle SD, `s_p`, from per-particle means. For two equal-sized independent lots, use at minimum `n ≥ 2(1.96s_p/1.0)²` particles per lot for a 95% CI on a 1 wt% difference; for 80% power use `n ≥ 2(1.96 + 0.84)²s_p²`. The confidence criterion gives approximately **8, 18, and 31 particles per lot** for `s_p = 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 wt%`, respectively. In practice, use **at least 30 and preferably 50–100 particles per lot**. Treat particles—not spectra or spots—as independent replicates. | Published powder studies support averaging many spectra but do not provide a universal particle count for atomized Al–Si. Automated powder EDS showed that cluster averaging improved agreement with expected composition, while real particle-to-particle variance depends on particle size, nucleation, and solute trapping. Sample size must therefore be calculated from observed particle variance rather than X-ray counts (pqac-00000031, pqac-00000064, pqac-00000071). |
| Powder sampling and splitting | Treat top, middle, and bottom as three separately collected strata. Take several increments across each stratum, combine them, gently homogenize without grinding, and reduce each composite with a **rotary sample divider** or validated multi-chute riffle splitter. Prepare at least **two independent splits and mounts per stratum**; do not scoop the analytical portion directly from the jar. | Reported subdivision variability was about 0.146% for rotary division and 1.01% for chute splitting, compared with 5.14% for scoop sampling and 6.81% for quartering. Vibration, funnel flow, and sifting can produce vertical size segregation in stored powder (pqac-00000037, pqac-00000039, pqac-00000040). |
| Particle-size stratification | Measure every analyzed particle’s equivalent diameter and assign predefined size bins. Compare lots both **within common size bins** and after reweighting to a common particle-size distribution. Also sieve representative bulk splits and analyze at least fine, middle, and coarse fractions independently. Include size and a lot-by-size interaction in the statistical model. | Fine droplets cool faster, undercool more, and exhibit greater solute trapping and less segregation; coarse droplets more readily form segregated structures. Published fine and coarse AlSi10Mg powders differed nominally in Si—8.56 versus 9.76 wt%—although this alone does not prove atomization segregation. Jar size segregation can therefore mimic a top-to-bottom Si gradient (pqac-00000065, pqac-00000068, pqac-00000069). |
| Statistical comparison | Fit a hierarchical model or mixed-effects ANOVA with **jar stratum or lot as the fixed effect**, size bin as a covariate, and split, mount, and particle as nested random effects. Report the top-minus-bottom estimate with its 95% CI, variance components, rejected-spectrum fraction, and sensitivity analysis with and without size adjustment. Claim resolution only when the 95% CI excludes zero and is sufficiently narrow to distinguish the specified 1 wt% contrast. | Repeated spectra on one particle improve counting precision but do not increase the number of independently sampled particles. Separating analytical repeatability, within-particle variation, between-particle variation, and split-to-split sampling variance prevents pseudoreplication and distinguishes a chemical gradient from particle-size segregation. |
| Confirmation by bulk method | Digest independent rotary-divided splits from every stratum and measure Si by **ICP-OES or ICP-MS**; alternatively, use calibrated bulk XRF. Run duplicate preparations, blanks, spikes, and a certified Al-alloy reference. Use XRD lattice parameter or phase fractions only as supporting evidence—not as the primary 1 wt% Si assay. Agreement between size-adjusted particle EDS and bulk chemistry is the strongest evidence of incomplete melt mixing. | EDS provides particle-resolved heterogeneity, whereas ICP and XRF provide mass-weighted bulk composition. Published gas-atomized Al–Si–Cu–Mg work used ICP-MS for post-atomization bulk chemistry and EDS/XRD for phase-scale segregation; direct same-split EDS-versus-ICP validation of atomized Al–Si powder remains sparse. Conventional XRD phase sensitivity is commonly several volume percent and cannot directly recover Si retained in supersaturated α-Al (pqac-00000041, pqac-00000058, pqac-00000062). |
