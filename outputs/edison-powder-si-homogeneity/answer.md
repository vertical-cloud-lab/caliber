# Resolving a 1 wt% Si Difference in Atomized Al–Si Powder by SEM-EDS: A Quantitative Assessment

## Overview

Distinguishing 5 from 6 wt% Si between two lots of gas-atomized hypoeutectic Al–Si powder at 95% confidence requires controlling five largely independent sources of uncertainty: (1) X-ray counting statistics, (2) particle geometry effects versus flat-specimen assumptions, (3) within-particle microstructural heterogeneity, (4) particle-to-particle compositional scatter, and (5) physical sampling of the powder jar. The following analysis draws on published literature to quantify each contribution and identify the dominant barriers.

## (1) Counting Statistics: Ziebold's Criterion and Modern SDD-EDS Performance

### Ziebold's Analytical Sensitivity

Ziebold (Anal. Chem. 39, 858–861, 1967; DOI: 10.1021/ac60252a028) defined the analytical sensitivity ΔC as the minimum concentration difference resolvable at a given confidence level, governed by Poisson counting statistics. For two measurements of a constituent at concentration C, the minimum resolvable difference at 95% confidence is approximately ΔC ≈ 2 × 1.96 × σ_C, where σ_C depends on the net peak counts N, the peak-to-background ratio, and the concentration itself. In the formulation retained by Goldstein et al. (Scanning Electron Microscopy and X-ray Microanalysis, Springer, 2018; DOI: 10.1007/978-1-4939-6676-9), the concentration detection limit is expressed as C_DL = (3√N_B / (N_S − N_B)) × C_S, where N_B is the background count beneath the peak, N_S is the total integrated count in the peak channel window, and C_S is the known standard concentration (newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 13-15, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 20-21).

### Counts Needed for Si Kα

For a constituent near 5–6 wt%, the relative statistical precision on a single measurement scales as approximately 1/√N, where N is the net characteristic peak count. To achieve a 1% relative counting precision (approximately ±0.05–0.06 wt% at 5–6 wt% Si), one needs roughly 10,000 net Si Kα counts per spectrum. For a cruder 5% relative precision (about ±0.25–0.30 wt%), approximately 400 net counts suffice. A two-sample test comparing 5 versus 6 wt% Si at 95% confidence purely from counting statistics requires modestly more than this, but the requirement is easily met: modern SDD-EDS systems routinely accumulate total spectrum integrals of 10⁶–10⁷ counts in 30–60 seconds of live time, yielding well over 10,000 Si Kα counts in an Al–5Si matrix (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 20-21, andersenUnknownyearascanningelectron pages 4-6).

### Modern SDD-EDS Precision and Repeatability

Newbury and Ritchie (J. Mater. Sci. 50, 493–518, 2015; DOI: 10.1007/s10853-014-8685-2) demonstrated that SDD-EDS with standards-based k-ratio analysis and NIST DTSA-II can match WDS precision and accuracy. On NIST SRM 470 (K411 glass), 20 randomly selected polished locations gave single-measurement relative standard deviations of 0.079% for Si (25.4 wt%), 0.11% for Mg (8.9 wt%), 0.089% for Ca (11.1 wt%), and 0.26% for Fe (11.2 wt%), with mean relative errors below 1.8% (newbury2011isscanningelectron pages 2-3, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 6-7). For NIST SRM 483 (Fe–3Si steel), Si at 3.22 wt% was measured at 3.13 wt% with 0.24% replicate relative precision over nine replicates (newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 5-6). Across 50 major/minor elemental determinations, 56% of relative deviations were within ±1% and 84% within ±2% (newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 4-5). Detection limits below 0.001 mass fraction (1000 ppm) were achieved within 500-second acquisitions (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 20-21, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 1-2). Spectra with approximately 1.5 × 10⁶ total counts yielded RSDs below 1–2% for oxides above 10 wt% and below 2% for most components between 1 and 10 wt% (andersenUnknownyearascanningelectron pages 12-15, andersenUnknownyearascanningelectron pages 4-6).

### Do Counting Statistics Dominate?

At 5–6 wt% Si, counting statistics are emphatically not the limiting factor for a standards-based measurement on a polished bulk specimen. With 10⁴–10⁵ net Si Kα counts, counting precision contributes less than ±0.1 wt% per measurement. The NPL measurement-uncertainty guide (Tong and Mingard, 2026; DOI: 10.47120/npl.mat135) identifies matrix-correction model uncertainty (approximately 1–7% relative for standards-based methods), specimen geometry and surface preparation, detector-efficiency calibration (especially below 2 keV), and beam-current stability (verified at 0.2% in a controlled test) as non-counting error sources that collectively dominate the uncertainty budget for major constituents (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 4-5, tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 21-24, tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 24-26, tong2026measurementuncertaintiesof pages 1-8).

## (2) Unpolished Loose Particles versus Polished Cross-Sections

### Particle Geometry Effects

The standards-based k-ratio protocol assumes both the unknown and standard are flat, polished, bulk targets at defined angles to the beam and detector. For spherical powder particles, three geometry-dependent effects alter Si Kα intensities relative to a bulk flat specimen:

**Mass effect:** Electrons scatter out through the sides and bottom of small particles, reducing X-ray generation. At 25 kV, Small (J. Res. NIST 107, 555, 2002; DOI: 10.6028/jres.107.047) measured approximately 50% intensity loss for 2 µm glass microspheres and 75% for approximately 1 µm particles. At 15 kV, the maximum loss fell to approximately 13% for a 1.8 µm sphere. At 10 kV, the loss was generally limited to about 10%, with particle/bulk ratios remaining near unity from approximately 0.9–18 µm (small2002theanalysisof pages 6-7).

**Absorption effect:** Si Kα photons (1.74 keV) are strongly absorbed by aluminum (just above the Al K-edge at 1.56 keV). In a spherical particle, the shorter average escape path length relative to a bulk specimen reduces self-absorption, causing the apparent Si Kα intensity to increase. At 25 kV, Si particle/bulk ratios reached approximately 1.4 and Mg ratios approximately 1.6 for microspheres larger than about 4 µm. These effects were substantially reduced at 15 kV and relatively flat at 10 kV (small2002theanalysisof pages 6-7, small2002theanalysisof pages 7-9).

**Combined geometry errors:** Newbury and Ritchie (SPIE 8036, 803602, 2011; DOI: 10.1117/12.881040) showed that all tested non-ideal geometries—scratches, fracture surfaces, chips, shards, and particles—produced strong deviations from the true composition, with errors up to a factor of ten compared with flat polished material. Normalized elemental errors remained within approximately 20% of the correct values only when the raw analytical total fell between 80% and 120% (newbury2011isscanningelectron pages 10-13). The raw analytical total (expected near 98–102% for a fully characterized specimen) is the primary diagnostic: normalization to 100% conceals these geometry-induced errors rather than correcting them (newbury2011isscanningelectron pages 13-16, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 5-6, newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 6-7).

### Normalization and Peak-to-Background Methods

The peak-to-background (P/B) ratio method exploits the approximate invariance of the characteristic-to-continuum intensity ratio between particle and bulk specimens of identical composition. The measured particle peak is normalized by the local continuum to produce a geometry-corrected, bulk-equivalent intensity (giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 3-5, giunto2025harnessingautomatedsemeds pages 4-6, goldstein2018quantitativeanalysisthe pages 30-31). Earlier implementations commonly achieved about 10–20% relative error for micron-scale particles (giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 1-3). A modern automated P/B workflow (Giunto et al., Nat. Commun. 17, 2026; DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-76633-x) validated on 74 single-phase powder samples achieved relative deviations generally within 5–10% of expected compositions on raw, irregularly shaped particles without embedding or polishing, using only one experimental standard per element. This is comparable to standardless analysis on polished bulk but weaker than standards-based analysis on a well-prepared flat mount (approximately 2–5% relative) (giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 14-15, giunto2025harnessingautomatedsemeds pages 7-10). The method incorporates corrections for continuum-generation differences (Zc, which can vary 20–500% between particle and standard as a function of average atomic number), absorption (Ac), backscattering (Rc), fluorescence (Fc), and a geometric absorption-path modification with fitted parameters z₀ and m (giunto2025harnessingautomatedsemeds pages 20-22).

### Recommended Particle Size Relative to Interaction Volume

At 5 kV, the electron interaction and X-ray generation volumes are much smaller than at 20 kV; for microspheres above about 1–2 µm, the particle effectively behaves as a bulk specimen. At 10 kV, particles larger than approximately 1.4 µm gave particle/bulk values within 0.9–1.1 for all elements in K411 glass, though the irregular-shard variation was approximately 8–12% relative. At 15 kV, mass effects only approached unity for particles larger than approximately 3 µm. At 20–25 kV, particles must exceed 5 µm to approach bulk behavior for high-energy lines (small2002theanalysisof pages 2-6, small2002theanalysisof pages 1-2, small2002theanalysisof pages 11-12, small2002theanalysisof pages 7-9, small2002theanalysisof pages 9-11). However, at 5 kV a contamination layer as thin as 5 nm of carbon reduced Ca k-ratios by 7%, and 10 nm reduced them by 13%, making low-overvoltage elements vulnerable (small2002theanalysisof pages 11-12). Thus, **10 kV represents a practical optimum** for particle analysis of Al–Si, offering low geometric errors while maintaining adequate overvoltage for Si Kα (overvoltage ratio ≈ 5.8).

## (3) Within-Particle Microstructural Heterogeneity

### Rapidly Solidified Al–Si Microstructure

Gas-atomized hypoeutectic Al–Si droplets solidify rapidly and develop highly heterogeneous microstructures within individual particles. Trivedi et al. (Acta Mater. 51, 289–300, 2003; DOI: 10.1016/s1359-6454(02)00226-4) demonstrated that latent-heat release produces recalescence, causing non-steady growth conditions during solidification of a single droplet, so that the interface velocity, thermal gradient, and cooling rate change from the nucleation site inward (trivedi2003dynamicalevolutionof pages 1-3, trivedi2003dynamicalevolutionof pages 3-4). Genau (OSTI 835374, 2004; DOI: 10.2172/835374) showed that Al–3Si and Al–6Si particles in the sub-micrometer to tens-of-micrometer range commonly exhibited three successive microstructural regions: an initial supersaturated α-Al solid solution formed under planar growth, followed by a segregate-free zone during recalescence, then breakdown to a cellular structure with increasing Si partitioning (genau2004microstructuraldevelopmentin pages 12-18).

### Dendrite Arm Spacing and Si Distribution

For gas-atomized AlSi10Mg, Vanzetti et al. (Metals 13, 1845, 2023; DOI: 10.3390/met13111845) reported a mean secondary dendrite arm spacing (SDAS) of 2.19 ± 0.56 µm, with Si concentrated in the interdendritic eutectic network while the α-Al dendrite cores were Si-depleted (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 7-10). Albu et al. (Addit. Manuf. 36, 101605, 2020; DOI: 10.1016/j.addma.2020.101605) found that eutectic Si in AlSi10Mg powder consisted of crystalline nanoparticles approximately 5–30 nm in size, with some amorphous Si nanoparticles (5–10 nm) embedded in the α-Al matrix (albu2020microstructureevolutionduring pages 3-4).

### Implications for Raster Size

A spot analysis placed on an α-Al cell will return a Si concentration far below the bulk particle mean, while a spot on eutectic Si will return a value far above it. To obtain a representative per-particle composition, the raster or summed area must span at least 5–10 SDAS lengths—approximately 10–20 µm for AlSi10Mg—and should ideally cover the entire particle cross-section on a polished mount. For particles in the 20–60 µm size range typical of atomized powder, a whole-cross-section raster on a polished section is feasible and strongly recommended. On loose particles, this corresponds to an area scan covering most of the visible particle face; for particles smaller than about 10 µm, the entire particle may fall within or near the interaction volume at 10–15 kV, effectively producing an area-averaged composition by default, though with attendant geometry-effect artifacts.

## (4) Sampling Statistics: Number of Particles and Size-Composition Correlations

### Particle-to-Particle Scatter

Individual atomized particles can differ in composition due to heterogeneous nucleation, stochastic cooling histories, and variable solute trapping. Genau (2004) documented that smaller particles are more likely to form supersaturated solid solution with suppressed segregation, while larger particles develop primary Si crystals and coarser eutectic structures (genau2004microstructuraldevelopmentin pages 60-66, genau2004microstructuraldevelopmentin pages 12-18, genau2004microstructuraldevelopmentin pages 29-37). No universal particle-to-particle standard deviation for Si in atomized Al–Si has been published. In the Giunto et al. (2026) automated EDS framework, 48.1% of elemental measurements across 74 diverse powder samples had standard deviations below 1 at% and 91.2% below 2 at%, but these values cover many chemistries and are not specific to atomized Al–Si (giunto2025harnessingautomatedsemeds pages 7-10).

### How Many Particles Per Lot?

For a two-sample t-test comparing lot means, the required number of particles per lot is n ≥ 2(z_α/2 + z_β)² × s_p² / Δ², where s_p is the between-particle standard deviation, Δ is the target difference (1 wt%), and z values correspond to the desired confidence (1.96 for 95%) and power (0.84 for 80%). If s_p = 1.0 wt% Si, approximately n ≈ 16 particles per lot provide 80% power; if s_p = 2.0 wt%, approximately n ≈ 63 are needed. Because s_p is unknown a priori, a pilot study of at least 30 particles per lot is advisable to estimate it. In practice, 50–100 particles per lot is a conservative and achievable target with automated SEM-EDS.

### Composition–Size Correlations

Published AlSi10Mg powders showed nominal differences in Si between fine and coarse fractions: Balbaa et al. (Addit. Manuf. 37, 101630, 2021; DOI: 10.1016/j.addma.2020.101630) reported 8.56 wt% Si for the fine fraction versus 9.76 wt% Si for the coarse fraction, though these were nominal compositions rather than direct measurements of atomization-induced segregation (balbaa2021roleofpowder pages 4-8). Genau (2004) established the physical basis: fine droplets cool faster, undercool more, and trap more solute into supersaturated α-Al, while coarse droplets solidify more slowly with greater equilibrium partitioning and Si-rich second-phase formation (genau2004microstructuraldevelopmentin pages 7-12, genau2004microstructuraldevelopmentin pages 12-18, genau2004microstructuraldevelopmentin pages 51-56). Thus, a real composition–size correlation is physically expected, though its magnitude for a specific atomization run must be measured empirically.

### Segregation in Containers and Sampling

Powder in a collection jar can undergo size segregation due to vibration, sifting, and funnel flow. Neikov and Yefimov (Handbook of Non-Ferrous Metal Powders, 3–62, 2019; DOI: 10.1016/b978-0-08-100543-9.00001-4) documented that funnel-flow conditions create stagnant peripheral zones and increased sifting segregation, while vibration drives fine particles downward and coarse particles upward (or vice versa depending on density and morphology) (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 26-28). If fine particles are enriched in the bottom of the jar and coarse particles near the top (or the reverse), and if Si differs by particle size as described above, then a top-versus-bottom sampling without size matching will produce an apparent composition difference that reflects size segregation rather than incomplete melt mixing.

The reliability of sample-splitting methods varies dramatically: quartering produces standard deviations of approximately 6.81%, scoop sampling 5.14%, chute splitting 1.01%, and rotary sample division only 0.146% (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 1-3, neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 3-4). **Rotary division is essential** for any quantitative comparison at the 1 wt% level. Multiple increments should be taken from at least 300 mm below the surface of each jar stratum (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 1-3).

## (5) Published Examples: EDS/EPMA versus Bulk Methods

### Particle-Level EDS on Powder

The most comprehensive published validation of quantitative SEM-EDS on raw powder is by Giunto et al. (Nat. Commun. 17, 2026; DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-76633-x), who demonstrated their automated P/B workflow on 74 single-phase powder samples spanning 38 elements. Overall relative deviations were within 5–10% for most elements, with 75.8% of cluster-centroid compositions within 5% and 93.6% within 10% relative deviation (giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 14-15, giunto2025harnessingautomatedsemeds pages 7-10). For multiphase and powder-mixture samples, k-means clustering resolved individual phase compositions and minority phases (giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 8-14). However, this work did not specifically benchmark against ICP-OES or XRF on the same sample splits.

### ICP-MS Composition of Gas-Atomized Al–Si

Vanzetti et al. (Metals 13, 1845, 2023; DOI: 10.3390/met13111845) reported ICP-MS chemical compositions for AlSi10Mg-based gas-atomized powders with notable scatter: the base AlSi10Mg alloy measured Si = 11.94 ± 1.96 wt% and Mg = 0.54 ± 0.33 wt% by ICP-MS on five unsieved samples (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 2-3). The large standard deviations likely reflect real particle-to-particle or subsample-to-subsample variation rather than ICP-MS instrument imprecision, since bulk ICP-OES for dissolved metals typically achieves 0.2–0.5 wt% resolution (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 12-13). EDS compositional mapping on the same powders confirmed Si in the eutectic network and Cu in interdendritic θ-phase channels, consistent with the ICP-MS bulk values, but no quantitative EDS–ICP comparison was presented (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 10-14, vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 7-10).

### Nickel-Based Alloy 625 Powder

Mostafaei et al. (Powder Technol. 333, 180–192, 2018; DOI: 10.1016/j.powtec.2018.04.014) compared gas-atomized and water-atomized alloy 625 powders using SEM-EDS, reporting that gas-atomized powder had composition closer to nominal. However, their compositional assessment relied on manufacturer-reported values and did not provide a rigorous EDS-versus-ICP validation (mostafaei2018comparisonofcharacterization pages 1-5, mostafaei2018comparisonofcharacterization pages 5-9).

### Where the Literature Is Silent

**Direct same-split EDS-versus-ICP or EDS-versus-XRF validation studies on atomized Al–Si powder are sparse to nonexistent.** Most published powder-characterization work reports EDS results alongside manufacturer-specified or separately measured bulk compositions, without presenting paired analytical comparisons on identically prepared splits. Similarly, no published study could be identified that specifically tested whether SEM-EDS on atomized Al–Si powder can reliably detect a 1 wt% Si difference between lots at a stated confidence level. The statistical framework for particle counting (how many particles, how to account for particle-to-particle scatter, hierarchical variance decomposition) is largely absent from the powder-metallurgy EDS literature and must be imported from environmental particle analysis, mineral liberation analysis, or clinical assay-validation frameworks.

The accuracy of XRD lattice-parameter or phase-fraction measurements for quantifying Si in rapidly solidified Al–Si is limited by the fact that conventional XRD phase sensitivity is commonly several volume percent and cannot directly recover Si retained in supersaturated α-Al solid solution (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 12-13).

### Key Conclusions

1. **Counting statistics are not the bottleneck.** With modern SDD-EDS, accumulating ≥10,000 net Si Kα counts per particle takes seconds, yielding counting precision well below ±0.1 wt% (newbury2015performingelementalmicroanalysis pages 20-21, newbury2019electronexcitedxraymicroanalysis pages 5-6).

2. **Particle geometry is the largest single analytical error source for loose particles.** Without correction, Si Kα k-ratios from unpolished spheres can be biased by 10–40% relative at 15–25 kV due to mass and absorption effects, especially for the strong Al K-edge absorption of Si Kα at 1.74 keV (small2002theanalysisof pages 6-7, newbury2011isscanningelectron pages 10-13). Polished cross-sections eliminate this problem; P/B methods reduce it to approximately 5–10% relative (giunto2026accuratesemedsquantification pages 14-15).

3. **Within-particle microstructural heterogeneity demands area-averaging.** With SDAS approximately 2 µm and Si concentrated in the eutectic network, spot analyses are not representative. Whole-cross-section rastering on polished mounts is optimal (vanzetti2023designandcharacterization pages 7-10, albu2020microstructureevolutionduring pages 3-4).

4. **Particle-to-particle scatter and particle-size-dependent composition are the dominant sources of between-lot variance.** The required number of particles depends on this scatter, which must be measured empirically; 50–100 particles per lot is a practical starting point (genau2004microstructuraldevelopmentin pages 12-18, balbaa2021roleofpowder pages 4-8).

5. **Physical sampling is critical.** Rotary sample division (0.146% SD) rather than scooping (5.14% SD) or quartering (6.81% SD) is essential. Size segregation in the collection jar can create spurious top-versus-bottom composition gradients that mimic incomplete mixing (neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 1-3, neikov2019powdercharacterizationand pages 26-28).

6. **Confirmation by ICP-OES or bulk XRF on independent splits is strongly recommended**, as no published validation has demonstrated that particle-level SEM-EDS alone can reliably resolve a 1 wt% Si difference in atomized Al–Si at 95% confidence.

In summary, the measurement is feasible but requires careful control of specimen preparation (polished sections preferred), beam voltage (10 kV for loose particles, 10–15 kV for polished sections), sufficient particle counts (≥50 per lot), proper powder sampling (rotary splitting), particle-size stratification, and independent bulk-chemistry confirmation. The dominant challenge is not counting statistics but rather the interplay of particle geometry artifacts, microstructural heterogeneity, particle-to-particle compositional scatter, and physical sampling bias.
