## State of the Art in Physically Accurate Dynamical Simulation of EBSD/Kikuchi Master Patterns (2020–2026)

### 1. The Established Dynamical Bloch-Wave + Monte Carlo Approach (EMsoft/EMsoftOO)

The foundational approach for quantitatively accurate EBSD master pattern simulation rests on many-beam dynamical electron diffraction theory, as initially demonstrated by Winkelmann et al. (2007) and subsequently implemented in the open-source EMsoft package by Callahan and De Graef (2013). The methodology operates on three key physical assumptions as described by Zhang et al. (2025): (1) the initial inelastic scattering event is separated from subsequent elastic and coherent diffraction; (2) small variations in observed intensity (i.e., pattern features) are prioritized over absolute intensities, permitting simplifications in the electron number density as a function of beam direction, energy, and depth; and (3) the reciprocity principle is applied to transform the backscatter geometry into an equivalent problem of a plane wave emitting from a point on the detector being scattered by the crystal lattice, enabling the Bloch-wave formalism to calculate the resulting wave function (zhang2025comparisonofkikuchi pages 1-6).

Inclusion of many crystal planes is achieved via Bethe perturbation theory, which partitions reflections into "strong" and "weak" beams—critical because SEM-based Kikuchi patterns subtend wide solid angles containing many overlapping bands (zhang2025comparisonofkikuchi pages 1-6). The Monte Carlo component models the energy and depth distribution of backscattered electrons in the interaction volume, providing a physically motivated weighting of the dynamical diffraction signal as a function of electron energy loss and escape depth. Pascal et al. (2018) formalized the energy-weighted dynamical scattering simulation for multiple SEM diffraction modalities, and this approach was extended by Singh et al. (2019) to 2D and 3D quasicrystals and by Zhu and De Graef (2020) to interaction volumes containing lattice defects.

EMsoftOO represents the object-oriented modernization of the EMsoft codebase (originally Fortran-based), announced by De Graef (2025). It retains the same underlying physics—multi-beam Bloch-wave dynamical scattering with Monte Carlo electron transport—while providing a more modular, maintainable software architecture. EMsoft/EMsoftOO remains the **de facto open-source standard** for generating physically accurate EBSD master patterns and is widely used in the community for dictionary indexing (DI), where each experimental pattern is compared against a dictionary of simulated patterns projected from the master pattern for all sampled orientations (kaufmann2020phasemappingin pages 1-2).

### 2. Alternative and Newer Formalisms

**Winkelmann's Bloch-wave implementation and commercial integration.** Winkelmann et al. (2021) published a comprehensive treatment of Kikuchi pattern simulations for both backscattered and transmitted electrons. Their Bloch-wave dynamical simulation code, which also uses a reciprocity-based approach with strong-beam approximations, is implemented within Oxford Instruments' AztecCrystal MapSweeper software. Zhang et al. (2025) used this implementation with thresholds of d_hkl > 0.4 Å and I_hkl > 10% of I_max for the strong-beam selection, generating 1000×1000-pixel stereographic projections of the diffraction sphere at a single primary energy (zhang2025comparisonofkikuchi pages 15-20). This approach differs from EMsoft's fuller Monte Carlo energy-depth coupling but is production-ready within the commercial EBSD ecosystem for template-based pattern matching.

**Multislice approaches.** The multislice algorithm—standard for TEM/STEM simulation—has been explored for EBSD geometry (Liu et al., 2016, compared two multislice methods for EBSD pattern simulation). However, the multislice method is fundamentally designed for forward-scattering (transmission) geometries and requires substantial adaptation for the wide-angle backscatter geometry of EBSD. Xiao et al. (2026) recently reported experimentally validated multislice simulations of electron diffraction patterns, though primarily for transmission geometries. To the authors' knowledge, no production-ready multislice code currently generates full dynamical EBSD master patterns.

**S-matrix and PRISM methods.** Scattering-matrix (S-matrix) approaches, as discussed by Ophus (2019) in the context of 4D-STEM, and the PRISM algorithm for accelerating STEM simulations, represent important algorithmic advances for transmission electron microscopy but have not been adapted to the EBSD backscatter master-pattern problem in production codes.

**DFT-informed scattering factors.** Susi et al. (2019) developed an efficient method for generating ab initio electrostatic potentials using the projector-augmented wave (PAW) formalism, enabling DFT-derived scattering factors that account for chemical bonding effects neglected by the independent atom model (IAM) (susi2019efficientfirstprinciples pages 1-3). The abTEM code (Madsen and Susi, 2021) integrates these DFT potentials with multislice simulations and supports both CPU and GPU acceleration (madsen2021theabtemcode pages 3-5, madsen2021theabtemcode pages 1-3). However, abTEM is explicitly a **transmission** electron microscopy simulation code—it supports HRTEM, STEM, CBED, 4D-STEM, and ptychography, but the retrieved evidence contains no documentation of EBSD backscatter master-pattern generation capability (madsen2021theabtemcode pages 10-12, madsen2021theabtemcode pages 12-15, madsen2021theabtemcode pages 15-17). DFT-informed potentials remain an area where future integration with Bloch-wave EBSD codes could refine accuracy for materials where bonding effects are measurable.

**Inelastic scattering treatments.** Mendis et al. (2020) and Vos and Winkelmann (2019) investigated the roles of multiple elastic and inelastic scattering and energy-resolved contrast in Kikuchi diffraction, contributing to the theoretical understanding of how thermal diffuse scattering, plasmon losses, and phonon scattering affect EBSD pattern formation. These treatments inform improvements to the energy weighting in dynamical simulations but have not yet been incorporated into a distinct production simulation code.

### 3. Machine-Learning / Neural-Network Surrogates and GPU-Accelerated Methods

Several groups have applied deep learning to EBSD pattern analysis, though primarily for **classification and indexing** rather than for replacing the physical forward model:

- **CNN-based phase mapping:** Kaufmann et al. (2020) demonstrated that convolutional neural networks (CNNs, specifically the Xception architecture) can autonomously recognize phases by crystal symmetry, chemistry, and lattice parameters from EBSD patterns, outperforming traditional Hough-based methods for phase discrimination in multiphase materials (kaufmann2020phasemappingin pages 2-3, kaufmann2020phasemappingin pages 1-2).

- **Few-shot transfer learning:** Kaufmann et al. (2021) showed that transfer learning from ImageNet pre-trained weights enables efficient EBSD pattern classification with far fewer training examples (few-shot learning), achieving 0.97 precision and 0.96 recall for space-group classification with substantially faster convergence than training from scratch (kaufmann2021efficientfewshotmachine pages 2-3, kaufmann2021efficientfewshotmachine pages 3-4).

- **Orientation extraction by CNNs:** Jha et al. (2018) applied CNNs for extracting grain orientations directly from polycrystalline EBSD patterns, and Ding (2022) further explored ML-based orientation determination (kaufmann2021efficientfewshotmachine pages 10-11).

- **GPU-accelerated indexing:** The pyEBSDindex package (Rowenhorst, Callahan, and Ånes, 2024) provides GPU-accelerated Radon-transform-based band detection and EBSD orientation determination, dramatically speeding up the indexing step. This tool is production-ready and open-source but is an **indexer**, not a master-pattern generator.

It is important to note that current ML approaches function as **surrogates for the search/classification step** of the EBSD workflow—they do not replace the authoritative generation of dynamical master patterns by physics-based simulation. No published work in the 2020–2026 window demonstrates a neural network that generates quantitatively accurate dynamical EBSD master patterns from first principles, though ML-based denoising and pattern enhancement (e.g., Andrews et al., 2023) improve the quality of experimental patterns fed into physics-based indexing pipelines.

Key observations:

- **kikuchipy** (Ånes et al.) is a mature, pip-installable Python package for EBSD pattern processing, background correction, and analysis. It can load and project master patterns generated by EMsoft but does **not itself contain a dynamical Bloch-wave simulation engine** for generating EBSD master patterns. It provides kinematical approximations and geometrical projections.

- **diffsims** (pyxem ecosystem) provides kinematical diffraction simulation objects and is useful for electron diffraction geometry but does not produce dynamical EBSD master patterns.

- **orix** (Johnstone et al., 2020) handles crystal orientations and symmetry operations, complementing the above tools.

- **pyEBSDindex** is focused on fast GPU-accelerated indexing, not pattern simulation.

- **abTEM** (Madsen and Susi, 2021) is the most physically sophisticated pure-Python simulation code, supporting multislice dynamical diffraction with DFT-informed potentials and GPU acceleration, but it is designed exclusively for TEM/STEM transmission geometries and does not currently support EBSD backscatter master-pattern generation (madsen2021theabtemcode pages 3-5, madsen2021theabtemcode pages 1-3).

- **pyEMsoft** provides Python bindings to EMsoft's Fortran-based dynamical simulation routines and is the closest route to dynamical EBSD master patterns from a Python interface, though it requires compiling the underlying EMsoft libraries.

In summary, as of 2026, **no pure-Python, pip-installable package independently generates dynamical EBSD master patterns**. The practical workflow remains: use EMsoft (or EMsoftOO) to generate master patterns, then use kikuchipy/orix/pyEBSDindex for downstream analysis, projection, and indexing.

### 5. Accuracy-vs-Cost Trade-offs for Dictionary Indexing of Metallic Alloys

For metallic alloys such as Al-Si systems, several practical considerations govern the choice of simulation approach:

- **Kinematical simulations** correctly predict Kikuchi band positions (governed by Bragg's law and crystal geometry) and can suffice for basic orientation indexing via Hough-transform methods in high-symmetry, single-phase materials. However, they fail to reproduce the relative intensities of bands, the fine structure at zone axes, and excess-deficiency (E/D) effects (zhang2025comparisonofkikuchi pages 1-6).

- **Dynamical simulations** are necessary for (a) resolving pseudosymmetry ambiguities (e.g., distinguishing tetragonal from cubic phases), (b) phase discrimination in multi-phase alloys where crystal structures are similar (e.g., distinguishing Al matrix from Si precipitates, or ferrite from martensite in steels), (c) high-resolution EBSD strain mapping that requires accurate simulated reference patterns, and (d) template-based pattern matching that relies on physically correct intensity distributions (kaufmann2020phasemappingin pages 1-2, zhang2025comparisonofkikuchi pages 25-30).

- **Computational cost:** Generating a single dynamical EBSD master pattern with EMsoft typically requires minutes to hours on modern hardware depending on the number of reflectors and angular resolution. Dictionary indexing then requires projecting this master pattern for O(10^5–10^6) orientations and comparing each to the experimental pattern, which is computationally intensive but has been made tractable by GPU acceleration and spherical indexing methods. For an Al-Si alloy, one would generate separate master patterns for the Al (FCC, space group 225) and Si (diamond cubic, space group 227) phases.

- **Practical recommendation:** For routine orientation mapping of well-known high-symmetry metallic phases, Hough-based indexing with kinematical band tables remains fast and adequate. When phase discrimination, pseudosymmetry resolution, or precision orientation refinement is needed—common in Al-Si alloys containing multiple intermetallic phases—dynamical simulation via EMsoft combined with dictionary or template-based indexing provides measurably superior accuracy at the cost of longer setup and computation times. Zhang et al. (2025) demonstrated that dynamical template matching using the Winkelmann Bloch-wave approach (implemented in AztecCrystal MapSweeper) successfully determined orientations across multiple Kikuchi diffraction geometries for Al samples, with reconstructed diffraction spheres closely matching the dynamical simulations, though some differences remained at zone axes and HOLZ rings (zhang2025comparisonofkikuchi pages 30-35, zhang2025comparisonofkikuchi pages 25-30).

### Summary

The state of the art for physically accurate dynamical EBSD simulation remains firmly grounded in the Bloch-wave + reciprocity + Bethe perturbation formalism, with EMsoft/EMsoftOO (De Graef group) and Winkelmann's implementation (now in Oxford AztecCrystal) as the two production-ready engines. Alternative formalisms—multislice, S-matrix, DFT-informed potentials—are mature for TEM but have not been adapted to production EBSD backscatter geometry. ML/CNN methods accelerate indexing and classification but do not replace the physics-based forward model. The Python ecosystem (kikuchipy, diffsims, pyEBSDindex) provides excellent analysis and indexing tools but relies on EMsoft for dynamical master-pattern generation. For metallic alloy applications, dynamical simulation is essential for phase discrimination and precision analysis, while kinematical methods remain adequate for routine high-symmetry orientation mapping.
