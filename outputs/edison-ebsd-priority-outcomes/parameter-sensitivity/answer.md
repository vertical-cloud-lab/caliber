# Sensitivity, uncertainty, and reproducibility of EBSD-derived microstructural measurements

## Executive conclusion

EBSD-derived quantities are **not intrinsic outputs of the orientation map alone**. Grain size/count, low-angle versus high-angle boundary fractions, KAM/GND, texture strength, and GBCD are conditional on spatial sampling, orientation precision, indexing, reconstruction, cleanup, censoring, and statistical weighting. The strongest quantitative evidence concerns grain size: plausible operator and software choices can change number-weighted results by **±26–100%**, while carefully constructed area-weighted measures typically vary by **±0.2–10%**. A standards-oriented workflow can achieve a combined expanded uncertainty of roughly **20% at k=2**, but uncontrolled workflows can differ far more. Formal uncertainty studies are substantially less mature for KAM/GND, LAGB/HAGB fractions, texture intensity, and GBCD. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 5-10, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 20-24)

## 1. Quantified sensitivity studies

### 1.1 Step size and mapped area

Step size affects both spatial resolution and the *effective* grain-reconstruction criterion. In microstructures with smooth intragranular rotation, the same total rotation is divided among more neighboring points at fine step size; coarsening can therefore make point-to-point misorientation exceed the reconstruction threshold and split a formerly connected grain. Coarse sampling can also disconnect narrow regions of non-convex grains. In an AM Hastelloy-X example, increasing step size from **3 to 6 μm** reduced the maximum reconstructed minimum-Feret diameter from **1250 to 970 μm**. Binning from **1.25 to 15 μm** produced a systematic decline in median and 75th-percentile minimum-Feret diameter. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 10-16)

ISO 13067-oriented guidance is approximately **one step per 10% of mean grain diameter**. A commonly used 20%-of-diameter rule can be inadequate for non-convex AM grains. Step size and boundary threshold are interdependent and must be held fixed for comparisons. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 16-20, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 20-24)

Field of view is at least as important as pixel density. Bootstrap analysis found approximately **10% sampling uncertainty at a field width of 15 grain lengths**, with the mean stabilizing above roughly seven grain lengths but outlier risk remaining. The proposed robust condition is about **20 grain lengths per field of view**, especially for elongated AM grains. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 20-24, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 10-16)

### 1.2 Grain-reconstruction misorientation tolerance

Increasing the reconstruction threshold merges boundaries and therefore increases mean grain size and decreases grain count. In the titanium round-robin reanalysis, changing the boundary threshold from **5° to 15°** increased mean size:

- **30.5 to 31.8 μm** at 0.5 μm step and 2 μm cutoff: **+4.3%**;
- **35.9 to 37.5 μm** at 3 μm step and 10 μm cutoff: **+4.5%**;
- **34.9 to 36.9 μm** in a tilted condition: **+5.7%**;
- **33.6 to 35.4 μm** in another 3 μm condition: **+5.4%**. (mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 23-26)

This parameter also directly determines LAGB/HAGB fractions: raising the grain threshold reclassifies or removes boundaries in the interval between the old and new thresholds. Thus a reported “LAGB fraction” is uninterpretable unless the lower noise cutoff, LAGB/HAGB dividing angle, reconstruction rule, and length/segment weighting are stated. For AM structures containing intragranular rotations, 10° has been recommended over 5° for identifying morphological grains, but that is a purpose-dependent convention rather than a universal physical boundary. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 20-24, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 1-5, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 10-16)

### 1.3 Minimum-grain cutoff and grain weighting

The minimum accepted grain size was the **largest identified source of interlaboratory variation** in the titanium round robin. Small grains disproportionately affect number-weighted means, and the effect depends on step size, indexing quality, and treatment of unindexed points. ISO 13067 and ASTM E2627 were reported as using markedly different exclusion scales—approximately **10 points** and **100 points**, respectively—so “standard-compliant” analyses can still differ unless the standard and implementation are specified. Cutoffs should be reported in both pixels and physical units. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 5-10, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 30-32, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 1-9)

The direction of bias is systematic: removing small grains lowers grain count and raises number-weighted mean grain size. In inter-software AM analyses, changing cleanup produced **±26–100%** changes in number-weighted metrics but only **±0.2–10%** in area-weighted metrics. Indexed-area-weighted median minimum-Feret diameter is consequently much more robust than a number mean. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 5-10)

In one AM dataset, indexed-area-weighted medians had half-ranges of only **±1.5% for MFD** and **±9.3% for grain area**. Conventional area-weighted medians varied by **±16%** and **±33%**, respectively, because field-size, edge, and cutoff effects were not fully represented. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 10-16)

### 1.4 Indexing fraction, confidence/fit thresholds, and cleanup

Cleanup sensitivity rises sharply as raw map quality declines. Estimated cleanup uncertainty was **±13% at 65% indexing**, compared with **±4% at 95% indexing**. Unindexed points are generally not missing at random; they concentrate at boundaries, deformed areas, fine features, and weak patterns. Depending on topology, they can fragment one grain into several or allow adjacent grains to be linked after filling. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 16-20)

The titanium round robin compared systems that either indexed every point with a confidence measure or left points unindexed when fit criteria failed. One reanalysis rendered points with **confidence index <0.1** unindexed. This illustrates why “indexing percentage” cannot be compared independently of the CI or fit threshold and indexing philosophy. Approximately 80% indexing was workable in that particular round robin despite a nominal 95% target, but it was not evidence that 80% is generally sufficient. (mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 30-32, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 23-26)

Grain dilation is explicitly topology-changing. One 3D GBCD workflow used **two iterations**, a **10-pixel minimum grain**, and reassigned groups below 10 pixels whose disorientation was below **5°** to an adjacent grain. Such processing erases small components and modifies boundary geometry; it is not neutral denoising. (rohrer2010derivinggrainboundary pages 2-4)

The standards-oriented limits reported in the AM study were that cleanup should alter results by no more than **5% under ISO 13067** or **10% under ASTM E2627**. These limits should be treated as acceptance checks, not proof that the cleaned map is unbiased. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 5-10)

Direct controlled, transferable numerical studies for **neighbor orientation correlation, neighbor pattern averaging, and vendor-specific CI standardization** were not found in the retrieved quantitative literature. Their expected directions are nevertheless clear: orientation-correlation filling and dilation remove isolated grains and shorten or move boundaries; neighbor-pattern averaging improves signal-to-noise but mixes patterns across boundaries unless boundary-aware, degrading spatial resolution and potentially suppressing narrow grains and LAGBs. Because the magnitude is dataset- and implementation-specific, it should be measured by raw-versus-processed parameter sweeps rather than assumed.

### 1.5 Indexing method and orientation precision: Hough, refinement, and HR-EBSD

Conventional Hough EBSD has reported absolute-orientation accuracy of roughly **2°** and relative misorientation precision of approximately **0.1–0.5°**. Cross-correlation HR-EBSD reaches about **0.006°**, an improvement of roughly two orders of magnitude. This difference changes the noise floor of every local-misorientation-derived quantity. (britton2018understandingdeformationwith pages 1-3)

For Burgers vector magnitude 0.3 nm, estimated measurable GND floors at **1 μm step** are above approximately **3×10¹³ m⁻²** for conventional Hough EBSD and **3×10¹¹ m⁻²** for HR-EBSD. At **100 nm step**, the corresponding floors rise to **3×10¹⁴** and **3×10¹² m⁻²** because curvature divides angular error by spatial separation. (britton2018understandingdeformationwith pages 1-3)

HR-EBSD also has analysis choices: reference and test patterns should remain within roughly **12°** for adequate overlap; normalized cross-correlation peak height below **0.3** indicates an unreliable result in simulations; and omitting rotational remapping can generate artificially high stresses. GND inversion is underdetermined and produces a lower-bound solution conditional on the selected dislocation types and optimization norm. (britton2018understandingdeformationwith pages 3-6)

The retrieved full-text evidence did not support a defensible universal numerical conversion between **Hough, dictionary indexing, and full-pattern orientation refinement** for grain size, texture intensity, or GBCD. The robust conclusion is narrower: full-pattern/cross-correlation refinement greatly reduces relative angular noise and therefore materially changes KAM/GND and near-threshold LAGB statistics, whereas grain topology will change mainly where Hough misindexing or angular error lies near the chosen reconstruction threshold. Method, pattern resolution/binning, refinement model, and quality rejection threshold must therefore be reported.

### 1.6 KAM and GND density

KAM is particularly parameter-dependent because it is an average over a user-defined neighborhood, normally after rejecting neighbor pairs above a chosen angle. Changing step size changes the physical kernel radius; changing neighbor order changes spatial smoothing; changing the exclusion angle changes whether boundaries and subboundaries enter the calculation; cleanup suppresses isolated angular outliers and therefore usually lowers KAM. GND density additionally scales as orientation gradient, approximately angular change divided by step length, so its noise floor rises as step size decreases unless angular precision improves proportionally. The Hough-versus-HR floors above quantify this effect. (britton2018understandingdeformationwith pages 1-3)

Consequently, KAM or GND values are not reproducible if only “KAM was calculated” is reported. Required information includes step size, neighbor shell/kernel and physical radius, boundary exclusion angle, whether zero/unindexed neighbors are omitted, indexing/refinement method and angular precision, smoothing or cleanup, Burgers vector, finite-difference scheme, assumed slip/dislocation systems, inversion norm, and whether the value is a lower bound. The retrieved corpus contained no mature interlaboratory uncertainty standard for KAM/GND comparable to ASTM E2627 or ISO 13067 for grain size.

### 1.7 Texture strength and ODF intensity

Texture has two separable sensitivities: orientation-measurement error and statistical/sectioning bias. Synthetic sectioning showed that even a truly uniform 3D ODF can acquire a nonuniform 2D EBSD ODF because elongated grains are more likely to be intersected when their long axes are near the section normal. This systematically inflates apparent fabric strength; it is not random angular noise. (currier2024towardsanintegrated pages 1-3, currier2024towardsanintegrated pages 9-12)

For repeated synthetic trials, the J-index decreased toward its asymptote as grain count increased; above approximately **2000 crystal orientations**, further sample-size effect was minimal. ODFs based on **3000 grains** could nevertheless retain morphology-driven cut bias. Kernel optimization also generated small “stair-step” changes in J-index as sample size changed, demonstrating that ODF bandwidth is itself a processing parameter. (currier2024towardsanintegrated pages 9-12)

Thus texture reporting should specify whether orientations are point-, grain-, or area-weighted; number of independent grains and points; ODF kernel/bandwidth and symmetry; section orientation; grain-shape anisotropy; and any smoothing or removal of low-quality points. Point weighting overrepresents large grains, while one-orientation-per-grain weighting gives each grain equal weight. Neither is universally correct; the estimand must be declared.

### 1.8 Boundary fractions and GBCD

LAGB/HAGB fractions are highly sensitive near both the angular-resolution floor and the chosen class boundary. Conventional Hough uncertainty of **0.1–0.5°**, and possible calibration errors of **2–3°**, can move substantial boundary length among low-angle bins; older work found misorientations ≥20° generally within 5° of truth, with larger relative errors at smaller angles. (muirhead2001themorphologicalcharacterisation pages 37-43, britton2018understandingdeformationwith pages 1-3)

Five-parameter GBCD adds sensitivity to boundary-plane reconstruction, registration, voxel aspect ratio, and angular binning. In a 3D Σ3 analysis, the coherent-twin peak was:

- **240 MRD** at 1:1 spacing without secondary alignment;
- **390 MRD** at 2:1 spacing without that alignment;
- **670 MRD** at 1:1 after secondary alignment;
- **1090 MRD** using both 2:1 spacing and secondary alignment, essentially matching **1100 MRD** from stereology. (rohrer2010derivinggrainboundary pages 4-5)

Changing discretization from 9 to 11 bins per 90° moved the peak to **3600 MRD**, because the ideal twin changed from lying on a bin boundary to lying within a bin. An independent length-based estimate gave **21.9% coherent twins**, while the approximately 1100-MRD reconstruction differed by more than 20% from the corresponding expected peak. GBCD peak intensity is therefore not reproducible without the full discretization and registration specification. (rohrer2010derivinggrainboundary pages 4-5)

## 2. Documented systematic bias

The best-supported directions of bias are:

1. **Minimum-size filtering:** removes fine grains, lowers grain count, and raises number-weighted grain size. It was the dominant source of variation in the titanium round robin. (mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 30-32, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 1-9)
2. **Dilation/neighborhood reassignment:** erases small islands, smooths boundaries, and merges them into larger neighbors; grain size rises and boundary length/count usually falls. The exact magnitude depends on map quality and iteration count. (rohrer2010derivinggrainboundary pages 2-4)
3. **Confidence/fit rejection without controlled filling:** creates non-random holes near boundaries and deformed regions; it may fragment grains. Filling those holes may instead bridge genuine boundaries. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 16-20, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 23-26)
4. **Coarser step size:** may miss small grains and narrow boundaries, but in continuously rotating AM grains it can also split grains, producing the observed decrease in reconstructed size. The sign is therefore microstructure-dependent, although the Hastelloy-X series showed decreasing size with coarsening. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 10-16)
5. **Higher reconstruction angle:** systematically merges low-angle domains and raises grain size by roughly 4–6% in the reported Ti test. (mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 23-26)
6. **Number weighting:** strongly overweights cleanup-generated or genuine small grains. Area-weighted measures reduced cross-cleanup variation from as much as ±100% to ≤10% in the reported comparison. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 5-10)
7. **Edge-grain exclusion:** without stereological correction, preferentially removes large grains and underestimates area-weighted size in small maps. ISO 13067 recommends Miles–Lantejoul-type correction, although it is difficult for irregular AM grains. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 5-10)
8. **Tilt/drift:** a 2° tilt error produced a predicted area-size factor of **0.95**. Under typical distortion most methods remained within **±0.5 ASTM G**, but strong drift produced changes up to **±2 G** for intercept methods and about **1 G** for planimetric methods; TPC/ASTM E2627-type approaches were most robust. (timberlake2026effectsofsem pages 14-17, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 23-26)
9. **2D sectioning of anisotropic grains:** inflates apparent ODF strength even when the true ODF is uniform. (currier2024towardsanintegrated pages 1-3, currier2024towardsanintegrated pages 9-12)
10. **Angular smoothing and cleanup:** generally lowers KAM/GND and LAGB content by suppressing short-wavelength orientation variations, but can create gradients at interfaces if orientations are inappropriately mixed. No universal correction factor exists.

## 3. Uncertainty quantification and statistical sufficiency

### Grain size

The most complete uncertainty framework treats:

- sampling variability from bootstrap subsampling as **Type A**;
- cleanup range and histogram/bin precision as **Type B**, often rectangular distributions;
- standard components combined in quadrature;
- expanded uncertainty reported with **k=2**.

Using this approach, a relative combined expanded uncertainty near **20%** was considered achievable for AM grain-size measurement. Sampling was usually the dominant component. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 20-24, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 10-16)

For generic grain-size stabilization, synthetic distortion work found curves flattening around **300–500 grains**. ASTM E112's traditional minimum is only **50 grains** for planimetric measurement or 50 intercepts, which is a minimum count rather than assurance of narrow uncertainty. (timberlake2026effectsofsem pages 14-17)

Reported size should include the distribution and uncertainty, not merely a mean. For skewed populations, lognormal/Weibull descriptions, medians, quantiles, and indexed-area-weighted cumulative distributions are preferable to assuming a Gaussian grain-number distribution. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 20-24, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 1-5, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 10-16)

### Texture

The available simulation study used **1000 repeated realizations** to estimate median and 95% critical J-index values. Approximately **2000 independent crystal orientations** were required before sample-count effects became small for the tested uniform ODFs. This is a more relevant sample-size concept than number of EBSD pixels because neighboring pixels within one grain are not independent orientation samples. (currier2024towardsanintegrated pages 9-12)

Confidence intervals for texture should therefore be generated by resampling grains or spatial blocks—not individual pixels—and should include ODF bandwidth uncertainty and, where grain shapes are anisotropic, sectioning/cut bias. Current tools often omit covariance among grain shape, size, and orientation, prompting calls for joint, open, validated stereological inversion. (currier2024towardsanintegrated pages 1-3)

### Misorientation, KAM, GND, and boundary statistics

For these outputs, a practical uncertainty budget should propagate angular precision through the spatial derivative and repeat the analysis over plausible cleanup, neighbor-kernel, and threshold choices. At minimum, report a noise floor such as the Hough/HR GND floors above. Because GND inversion is underdetermined and 2D EBSD measures only part of the curvature tensor, the resulting density is normally a model-dependent lower bound, not a complete dislocation-density measurement. (britton2018understandingdeformationwith pages 1-3, britton2018understandingdeformationwith pages 3-6)

Formal confidence intervals and minimum-grain requirements for KAM, GND, and LAGB/HAGB fractions remain an important literature gap. Spatial block bootstrap, repeated-map designs, and sensitivity envelopes are more defensible than pixel-wise standard errors.

## 4. Interlaboratory and inter-software reproducibility

The VAMAS/ISO-oriented titanium exercise included laboratories from **eight countries**, including three equipment manufacturers, five national metrology institutes, one university, and two industrial laboratories. Four of 12 laboratories produced significantly different results. After outlier removal, between-laboratory reproducibility standard deviations were **1.98 μm** for equivalent-circle diameter and **1.15 μm** for linear intercept. Equivalent-circle values averaged approximately **10% higher** than intercept values. (mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 30-32, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 1-9, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 9-11)

Equivalent-circle reproducibility was much poorer: reported reproducibility limits were **R=5.79 μm** for equivalent circle and **R=3.36 μm** for linear intercept, a roughly 70% difference. The causes were magnification calibration, tilt correction, scan geometry/drift, insufficient mapped area, step size, cleanup, and especially minimum-grain cutoff. Within-laboratory repeatability was substantially better than between-laboratory reproducibility. (mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 1-9, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 23-26)

An earlier multi-material comparison involving up to nine laboratories also found that only four performed consistently well; identified determinants were SEM resolution, EBSD camera, preparation, and operator skill. (mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 9-11)

The newer AM comparison applied a common 10° threshold across AZtecCrystal, OIM Analysis, and MTEX. It found that implementation differences in cleanup and grain-area calculation remain consequential, particularly for number-weighted measures. Area-weighted median MFD was substantially more software-robust. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 5-10)

For GBCD, the same dataset changed from **240 to 1090 MRD** solely through spacing/registration choices and to **3600 MRD** through angular discretization. This is effectively a software/protocol reproducibility warning even though it was not a multi-laboratory round robin. (rohrer2010derivinggrainboundary pages 4-5)

No broadly adopted benchmark or round robin was found that simultaneously compares vendors and laboratories for KAM, GND, LAGB/HAGB fractions, neighbor cleanup, and dictionary-versus-Hough indexing. That absence is itself important: grain-size metrology is relatively mature, while deformation and boundary-character metrics remain mostly method-specific.

## 5. Standards, consensus, and minimum reproducible reporting

ASTM E2627 and ISO 13067 primarily standardize EBSD grain-size measurement, not the full range of KAM/GND, texture, or GBCD analyses. From the standards-oriented studies, the minimum reporting package should include:

1. **Specimen and geometry:** preparation route, analyzed plane/direction, accelerating voltage, current, working distance, nominal and calibrated tilt, scan orientation, distortion/drift correction, and magnification calibration.
2. **Map sampling:** physical map dimensions, grid geometry, step size, field count and selection procedure, indexed fraction, number of points, grains, and edge grains.
3. **Pattern/indexing details:** detector/camera, pattern resolution and binning, exposure, Hough/dictionary/refinement/HR method, phase files, CI/fit/peak thresholds, and angular precision or empirical repeatability.
4. **Cleanup audit trail:** every operation in order, iteration count, neighborhood/kernel, CI or fit criterion, dilation/correlation/fill rule, whether patterns or orientations were averaged, and the fraction of points whose orientation changed. Raw and cleaned maps should both be retained.
5. **Grain reconstruction:** misorientation threshold, point-to-point versus grain-mean rule, treatment of pseudosymmetry/twins, connectivity, minimum-grain cutoff in points and physical units, removed grain count and area fraction, and edge-grain handling.
6. **Statistic:** number-, area-, or point-weighting; equivalent-circle, intercept, Feret, or area metric; complete distribution/quantiles; histogram bins or KDE bandwidth; and uncertainty coverage factor.
7. **KAM/GND:** physical kernel size and neighbor shell, angular cutoff, boundary exclusion, gradient operator, Burgers vector, slip/dislocation basis, inversion norm, smoothing, and detection/noise floor.
8. **Boundary statistics/GBCD:** angular noise cutoff; LAGB/HAGB threshold; length-, segment-, or count-weighting; CSL tolerance; line simplification; bin/discretization; voxel aspect ratio; serial-section registration; and uncertainty/sensitivity analysis.
9. **Texture:** number of independent grains, grain/point/area weighting, specimen and crystal symmetry, ODF kernel/bandwidth, section orientation, texture-strength definition, and confidence interval method.

The strongest consensus is that post-processing must be treated as part of the measurement model. Step size and reconstruction threshold must be reported together; cutoff and cleanup effects should be quantified; cleanup-induced changes should remain within the applicable standard's limits; field-of-view sampling should be included in uncertainty; and raw data plus scripts or complete software recipes should accompany results wherever possible. (tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 5-10, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 16-20, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 20-24, mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 9-11)

The literature explicitly motivates more objective workflows. The titanium round robin demonstrated that operator-selected cutoffs and cleanup dominate between-lab variation. The AM study advocates standardized, software-robust, indexed-area-weighted statistics with bootstrap uncertainty. The texture study calls for open, documented, validated tools that jointly correct grain shape, size, and orientation and provide uncertainty rather than applying separate hidden-assumption corrections. (mingard2012determinationofgrain pages 30-32, tong2026towardsstandardisationof pages 20-24, currier2024towardsanintegrated pages 1-3)

## Bottom line

- **Grain size/count:** highly sensitive unless step size, threshold, cutoff, cleanup, field size, edge treatment, and weighting are fixed. Number-weighted values may change by order-unity amounts; robust area-weighted medians can often be held within about 10% processing sensitivity.
- **Texture/ODF:** orientation precision matters, but independent-grain count, bandwidth, weighting, and 2D sectioning bias can be equally important. Around 2000 independent orientations were needed for stable J-index in the cited synthetic tests.
- **KAM/GND:** among the least transferable EBSD metrics. Conventional versus HR angular precision changes the GND floor by about two orders of magnitude, and step size changes it inversely.
- **LAGB/HAGB:** intrinsically threshold- and precision-dependent; cleanup and angular smoothing preferentially suppress LAGBs.
- **GBCD:** can change by factors of several solely from registration, voxel aspect ratio, and binning; sharp special-boundary peaks require full protocol reporting.
- **Reproducibility:** achievable but not automatic. The Ti round robin had 4/12 significant laboratory outliers; controlled grain-size workflows can approach roughly 20% expanded uncertainty, whereas comparable validated uncertainty frameworks are still lacking for KAM/GND and boundary-character metrics.
